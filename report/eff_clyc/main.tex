\begin{abstract}
Here I compare simulated and measured full-energy peak efficiencies for the CLYC2 detector at nominal source-to-detector distances of \SI{200}{mm}, \SI{300}{mm}, and \SI{400}{mm}. For the simulations, I ran isotropic Geant4 jobs with $10^7$ gammas per point and integrated the full-energy peak in a $\pm\SI{2}{\percent}$ total-energy window. For the measurements, I took the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 spectra, subtracted the \SI{600}{s} environmental background, and fitted each peak with a Gaussian plus linear background over $\pm1.5$ FWHM (without using any external efficiency table for the distance comparison). At \SI{661.64}{keV} the simulation/measurement ratio is around 0.98--1.03, and at \SI{1332.51}{keV} it is 0.92--1.11. That being said, at \SI{1173.23}{keV} the simulation comes out 19--36\% higher than the measurement. Because the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line, the Gaussian peak area has about a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty, however the observed excess is quite a bit larger than that: the measured \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} ratio is 1.00--1.04, compared to 1.18 in the simulation. Since cascade summing is negligible at these distances, it seems that this line-dependent excess likely comes from the Co-60 source geometry or the detector response model. As a separate check, I also compared the \SI{2}{cm} calibration file against a radioactive-decay simulation where the source puck is placed flush against the detector front face. This flush simulation matches reasonably well at \SI{46.54}{keV}, \SI{661.64}{keV}, and \SI{1408.01}{keV} -- as well as at \SI{59.54}{keV} once pulse clustering is applied -- while the \SI{121.78}{keV}, \SI{344.28}{keV}, and \ce{^{60}Co} lines still remain high. Fitting the \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines gives a detector energy resolution of $R(E)=(123.9\pm0.4)/\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\%$ (i.e., FWHM $=1.239\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\mathrm{keV}$).
\end{abstract}

\section{Geometry and Solid Angle Efficiency Bound}

In the Geant4 model (\texttt{GeometryCLYC.cc}), the detector uses a cylindrical CLYC crystal of diameter \SI{25}{mm} and length \SI{25}{mm} (radius $r=\SI{12.5}{mm}$, density \SI{3.31}{g\,cm^{-3}}) inside a stepped aluminium casing. The front face of the crystal is recessed by \SI{2}{mm} behind the outer front surface of the detector casing ($z_{\mathrm{recess}}=\SI{2}{mm}$), with a \SI{0.5}{mm} aluminium entrance window and a \SI{1.5}{mm} Teflon reflector cap in between. Behind the \SI{29}{mm} snout, the wider detector body and PMT are enclosed by a \SI{0.64}{mm}-thick MuMetal magnetic shield. I modeled the calibration sources as isotropic point sources sitting at the centre of an epoxy puck of radius \SI{10}{mm}, thickness \SI{2}{mm}, and density \SI{1.16}{g\,cm^{-3}} (\ce{H32C15N2O4}).

For a given nominal source-to-detector distance $d_{\mathrm{nom}}$, the actual distance from the source to the crystal front face is therefore $d=d_{\mathrm{nom}}+\SI{2}{mm}$. Figure~\ref{fig:setup} shows the axial setup along with the solid-angle cone.

\subsection{Solid angle and geometric acceptance}
For an isotropic point source emitting into $4\pi$, the solid angle subtended by the circular crystal entrance face of radius $r$ at distance $d$ along the detector axis is
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(d)=2\pi\left(1-\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right),
  \label{eq:solid-angle}
\end{equation}
so the corresponding geometric acceptance is
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_\Omega(d)=\frac{\Omega(d)}{4\pi}
  =\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right)\times100\%.
  \label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
For the source distances considered here, this gives:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_\Omega =
  \begin{cases}
    \SI{0.0955}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{200}{mm} \ (d=\SI{202}{mm}),\\
    \SI{0.0428}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{300}{mm} \ (d=\SI{302}{mm}),\\
    \SI{0.0242}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{400}{mm} \ (d=\SI{402}{mm}),\\
    \SI{38.33}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{1}{mm} \ (d=\SI{3}{mm})\quad\text{(flush)}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:omega-values}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Maximum efficiency bound and intrinsic efficiency}
We can factorize the total full-energy peak efficiency as
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon=\epsilon_\Omega(d)\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}(E_\gamma,d),
  \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\in[0,1]$ is the intrinsic efficiency -- i.e., the probability that a gamma entering the crystal deposits all of its energy. Since $\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\le1$, the geometric acceptance sets a strict upper bound on the efficiency:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon\le\epsilon_{\max}(d)=\epsilon_\Omega(d).
  \label{eq:eff-max}
\end{equation}

\section{Calculation Methods}

\subsection{Measured efficiency}
Some notes about the experimental data and analysis: the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 spectra were converted from CNF to ROOT using \texttt{cnf2root}, which writes out the channel-count histogram, the calibrated energy histogram, and the acquisition metadata (live time and energy calibration). In the analysis I kept the native $\sim\SI{1.96}{keV}$ energy bins, since rebinning to \SI{2}{keV} by bin centre would alias the two grids.

First, I subtracted the \SI{600}{s} environmental background spectrum bin by bin. Of course, this removes room and cosmic background, however it does not remove the source-induced Compton continuum. I then fitted each full-energy peak with a Gaussian plus linear background over $\pm1.5$ FWHM around the observed peak (i.e., \SI{1113.5}{keV}--\SI{1233.0}{keV} for the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line, and \SI{1264.4}{keV}--\SI{1400.6}{keV} for the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line), taking the Gaussian integral as the measured peak area.

From this, the measured efficiency is given by
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}{A_{\mathrm{exact}}P_\gamma t_{\mathrm{live}}/100}\times100\%,
  \label{eq:eff-exp}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is the source activity on the measurement date, $P_\gamma$ is the gamma-ray emission probability in percent, $t_{\mathrm{live}}=\SI{600}{s}$, and $N_{\mathrm{peak}}$ is the Gaussian peak area (with its statistical uncertainty taken directly from the fit).

For the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line, the peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line. As a cross-check, doing a local sideband integration over the $\pm1$ FWHM peak region (with sidebands at 1.2--1.8 FWHM) gives a peak area that differs from the Gaussian-plus-linear fit by about \SI{10}{\percent}. I treat this \SI{10}{\percent} difference as the background-model systematic for the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line. For the cleaner \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines, the two methods agree to within about \SI{1}{\percent}.

\subsection{Simulated efficiency}
The Geant4 simulations use \texttt{G4EmStandardPhysics\_option4}. For each energy and distance point, I generated $10^7$ monoenergetic gammas isotropically from the centre of the epoxy puck and summed all energy deposited in the CLYC crystal per event. Events are counted in the full-energy peak if they fall within a $\pm2\%$ window:
\begin{equation}
  0.98E_\gamma\le E_{\mathrm{dep}}\le1.02E_\gamma.
  \label{eq:sim-window}
\end{equation}
The simulated efficiency and its statistical uncertainty are then simply
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}{N_{\mathrm{inc}}}\times100\%,
  \qquad
  \sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}=\frac{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}}{N_{\mathrm{inc}}}\times100\%,
  \label{eq:eff-sim}
\end{equation}
with $N_{\mathrm{inc}}=10^7$. Note that for this efficiency table I did not apply detector resolution smearing -- in the monoenergetic simulation the full-energy peak is just a delta line, so the $\pm2\%$ window already captures the entire peak.

To compare simulation and measurement, I use the ratio $R$ and statistical pull:
\begin{equation}
  R=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{pull}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}}
  {\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{exp}}^2}}.
  \label{eq:ratio-pull}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Flush source response}
In the flush geometry, the source puck sits directly against the detector front face. This places the point source at the puck centre \SI{1}{mm} away from the detector front plane, and \SI{3}{mm} from the crystal front face. Here I tracked the full decay chains using \texttt{G4RadioactiveDecayPhysics} with parent ions placed at rest at the puck centre ($N_{\mathrm{decays}}=10^6$ per source). In the default runs the long-decay threshold was set to \SI{1e60}{y}, and in the analysis I merged crystal hits whose leading-edge times are separated by at most the \SI{1}{\micro\second} CLYC resolving time. As a check on late decay-chain contributions, setting the threshold to \SI{1e4}{y} stops the \ce{^{241}Am} chain at \ce{^{237}Np} and leaves the \ce{^{137}Cs}, \ce{^{210}Pb}, \ce{^{152}Eu}, and \ce{^{60}Co} results unchanged. The simulated peak efficiency is then calculated from the clustered pulse counts as
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}(E_\gamma)=
  \frac{N_{\mathrm{pulse}}(E_\gamma)}{N_{\mathrm{decays}}P_\gamma(E_\gamma)}
  \times100\%,
  \label{eq:flush-eff}
\end{equation}
with the statistical uncertainty taken from $N_{\mathrm{pulse}}$.

\subsection{The \SI{2}{cm} calibration file}
The existing calibration file \texttt{eff\_clyc1.csv} is labelled as a \SI{2}{cm} measurement. It does not have a detector column, so I treat it as CLYC1-only. Its printed numbers match Eq.~\eqref{eq:eff-exp} without an extra factor of \num{1000}, and at lower energies (\SI{32}{keV}--\SI{121}{keV}) the efficiencies are quite high, ranging from \SI{15.4}{\percent} to \SI{23.9}{\percent}. Since those values are way above the geometric acceptance at \SI{2}{cm} -- and the file does not document the exact source reference point or detector identity -- I compared them in Sec.~\ref{sec:source_response} against a simulation with the source puck flush against the detector face.

Now, some analysis of the distance scan:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item For \ce{^{137}Cs} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, the simulation/measurement ratio is 0.98--1.03 across the three distances, with pulls between $-0.4$ and $+0.8\sigma$.
  \item For \ce{^{60}Co} at \SI{1332.51}{keV}, the ratio is 0.92--1.11, with pulls between $-0.8$ and $+1.8\sigma$.
  \item For \ce{^{60}Co} at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, however, the ratio is 1.19--1.36, with pulls between $+2.0$ and $+4.4\sigma$.
\end{enumerate}

The \SI{1173.23}{keV} line is clearly the outlier here. In the data, the \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} efficiency ratio is around 1.00--1.04, whereas the simulation predicts 1.18. Even accounting for the \SI{10}{\percent} background-model systematic on the \SI{1173.23}{keV} Gaussian area from the sideband check, that uncertainty does not explain away the discrepancy. And because the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line agrees within 0.92--1.11, this is a line-dependent difference rather than a simple overall normalization offset.

I do not think this is coming from Co-60 cascade summing either: at \SI{200}{mm}--\SI{400}{mm}, the total interaction probability per gamma in the crystal is only about $3.3\times10^{-4}$, so the probability for both cascade gammas to interact in the same decay is around $10^{-7}$ and the \SI{2505.74}{keV} sum peak is negligible. Instead, the \SI{1173.23}{keV} excess seems to point to an energy-dependent effect in either the actual Co-60 source capsule/holder geometry, the active crystal volume, or the reflector/casing model -- though with the current dataset alone it is hard to say which one is responsible.

\section{Experimental Spectra and Peak Integration}
\label{sec:peak_integration}

As noted above, the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 runs are the only measured spectra used in this report. Following the CNF-to-ROOT conversion and \SI{600}{s} environmental background subtraction, the $\pm1.5$ FWHM Gaussian-plus-linear fits give the measured efficiencies listed in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} (for example, the \SI{1173.23}{keV} fit window is \SI{1113.5}{keV}--\SI{1233.0}{keV}). Comparing those Gaussian areas against local sideband integration over the same peak region gives agreement to within about \SI{1}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV}, and about a \SI{10}{\percent} difference at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, which I use as the continuum-model systematic.

To look a bit more closely at the background shape under the Co-60 lines, Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co_fits} shows diagnostic Gaussian-plus-quadratic-background fits to the \SI{200}{mm} Co-60 spectra. While Table~\ref{tab:comparison} uses the linear background model for consistency across all runs, using a quadratic background here accounts a bit better for the curved Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line sitting underneath the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak. With the quadratic background, the fitted simulation/experiment peak-area ratios are 1.21 at \SI{1173.23}{keV} and 1.04 at \SI{1332.51}{keV} (compared to 1.30 and 1.11 in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}); the shift between the two fits reflects the background-model uncertainty on the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak.

\section{Detector Resolution and Simulated Spectrum Comparison}
\label{sec:resolution}

\subsection{Resolution model}
From the same Gaussian-plus-linear fits at 200, 300, and 400 mm, I extracted the FWHM of the \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{1332.51}{keV} peaks. The widths are quite consistent across all three distances; Table~\ref{tab:resolution} lists the mean values and the standard error of the mean. This gives an energy resolution \(R=\mathrm{FWHM}/E\) of \SI{4.80}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{3.40}{\percent} at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{3.41}{\percent} at \SI{1332.51}{keV}. Because the \SI{1173.23}{keV} width is biased a bit by the broad Compton background underneath the peak, I excluded it from the resolution fit and fitted only the two cleaner lines (\SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV}), which gives
\[
  \mathrm{FWHM}(E)=\SI{1.239}{\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}}\,\SI{}{\kilo\electronvolt},
  \qquad
  R(E)=\frac{123.9\pm0.4}{\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}}\,\%.
\]
The \SI{1173.23}{keV} point sits \SI{6.2}{\percent} below this curve, which is consistent with what I would expect from the background-model bias.

I only use this resolution curve to smear the simulated spectra for visual/spectral comparison: for each event with deposited energy \(E_{\mathrm{dep}}\), the energy is drawn from a Gaussian with mean \(E_{\mathrm{dep}}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma(E_{\mathrm{dep}})=\mathrm{FWHM}(E_{\mathrm{dep}})/2.3548\). \subsection{Simulated and experimental spectra at 200 mm}
\label{sec:spectra_200mm}

Now to the plots comparing the full spectra at \SI{200}{mm}. For these, I ran $10^7$-event monoenergetic simulations with the GPS emission restricted to a 15 degree half-angle cone around the detector axis to get better statistics in the crystal. Restricting the emission to the cone boosts the number of gammas reaching the crystal by the inverse of the cone solid-angle fraction, $f=(1-\cos15^\circ)/2=0.017037$, corresponding to an effective isotropic incident count of $N_{\mathrm{inc,eff}}=N_{\mathrm{gen}}/f=\num{5.87e8}$. As a sanity check, the cone runs reproduce the isotropic full-energy peak efficiencies within about \(1\sigma\): \SI{0.00871}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00843}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{0.00373}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00384}{\percent} at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{0.00308}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00326}{\percent} at \SI{1332.51}{keV}. Since the 15 degree cone cut only drops wide-angle gammas that would have scattered into the crystal from outside the cone (which contribute mainly to the continuum), the full-energy peak comparison remains quantitative while the low-energy continuum comparison is more qualitative.

Both the smeared and unsmeared simulated spectra use the exact same events and scaling. With the expected number of emitted gammas given by
\[
  N_{\mathrm{emit}}=A_{\mathrm{exact}}P_\gamma t_{\mathrm{live}}/100,
\]
the histogram scale factor is $N_{\mathrm{emit}}f/N_{\mathrm{gen}}$. For Cs-137 at \SI{200}{mm}, $N_{\mathrm{emit}}=\num{2.834e8}$ and the scale factor is 0.4828. For Co-60, I summed the \SI{1173.23}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} runs, scaling each by its respective $N_{\mathrm{emit}}$ (\num{7.031e8} and \num{7.032e8}, scale factor 1.198). As before, the unsmeared histogram fills $E_{\mathrm{dep}}$ directly, while the smeared histogram draws from a Gaussian with $\sigma(E_{\mathrm{dep}})=\mathrm{FWHM}(E_{\mathrm{dep}})/2.3548$. Since the two lines are generated in separate monoenergetic runs, the \SI{2505.74}{keV} coincidence sum peak is not present in the simulation.

For Cs-137 at \SI{200}{mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_cs}), the resolution-smeared simulation matches the \SI{661.64}{keV} peak quite well: in the \(\pm2\%\) energy window, the ratio of background-subtracted data counts to the smeared simulation is 0.955, and the smeared simulated peak area is only about \SI{5}{\percent} above the measured Gaussian area. At lower energies the simulation sits below the measured continuum, which is expected since the experimental spectrum includes room scatter and environment-scattered radiation that are not in the monoenergetic model. Also, the measured \SI{200}{mm} Cs-137 spectrum does not show a resolved \SI{32}{keV} Ba X-ray peak -- likely because the source encapsulation absorbs those X-rays, whereas the monoenergetic run only generates the \SI{661.64}{keV} gamma anyway. So the \SI{661.64}{keV} peak itself is the main quantitative benchmark here.

For Co-60 at \SI{200}{mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co}), Table~\ref{tab:comparison} gave $\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}/\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}=1.30$ at \SI{1173.23}{keV} and 1.11 at \SI{1332.51}{keV}. Note that the bin-by-bin ratio plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co}(b) looks a bit different near \SI{1173.23}{keV} because it is an interval ratio rather than a background-subtracted peak-area ratio: the \(\pm2\%\) window at \SI{1173.23}{keV} is \SI{46.9}{keV} wide (\num{1.18} FWHM) and includes Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line. Specifically, the background-subtracted data integral in that window is \num{23396} counts (vs.\ \num{20800} in the Gaussian peak), whereas the smeared simulation has \num{24306} counts in the window (and about \num{26200} in the full-energy peak). Taking the raw window ratio gives \num{0.963}, whereas comparing the actual full-energy peak counts gives $20{,}819/26{,}210=0.79$ (i.e., a simulation/measurement ratio of \num{1.26}, consistent with the 1.30 in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). Figure~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co_fits} shows the explicit peak-plus-background decompositions, where the fitted simulation/experiment peak ratios are 1.21 and 1.04. Even with the \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty from Sec.~\ref{sec:peak_integration}, the \SI{1173.23}{keV} simulation still sits high, while the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line agrees within the peak-integration uncertainty. A couple of other small features in the measured Co-60 spectrum are worth noting: there are weak lines at \SI{141.8}{keV} and \SI{222.1}{keV} that are not from Co-60 (and are absent in the simulation), and the \SI{2505.74}{keV} sum peak is absent in the monoenergetic simulation as well. That being said, at \SI{200}{mm} the sum peak is expected to be around 5 counts (with a summing-out probability below \SI{0.05}{\percent} at 200--400 mm given the $3.3\times10^{-4}$ single-gamma interaction probability), so cascade summing cannot explain the \SI{1173.23}{keV} excess.

Overall, the flush simulation behaves as expected physically: every point stays below the \SI{38.33}{\percent} geometric ceiling, and the \SI{32}{keV} efficiency (\SI{15.90}{\percent}) drops below the \SI{46.54}{keV} and \SI{59.54}{keV} points because photoelectric absorption inside the \SI{2}{mm} epoxy puck attenuates the \SI{32}{keV} X-ray. Above \SI{122}{keV}, the efficiency falls off smoothly with energy down to \SI{0.431}{\percent} at \SI{1408}{keV}.

Just to recap the geometry here: placing the \SI{2}{mm}-thick puck flush against the detector front face puts the source (at the puck centre) \SI{1}{mm} in front of the casing surface, and since the crystal face is recessed by \SI{2}{mm}, the source-to-crystal distance is \(d=\SI{3}{mm}\). Plugging \(d=\SI{3}{mm}\) into Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega} gives \(\epsilon_\Omega(3\,\mathrm{mm})=\SI{38.33}{\percent}\). Comparing the flush simulation to the printed CSV numbers in Fig.~\ref{fig:source_eff} and Table~\ref{tab:source_eff}:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item With the \SI{1}{\micro\second} pulse clustering applied, the flush simulation agrees reasonably well with the CSV at \SI{46.54}{keV}, \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{1408.01}{keV}, and \SI{59.54}{keV} (\(R=\num{1.058}\), \num{1.100}, \num{1.079}, and \num{1.202}).
  \item At \SI{32.00}{keV}, the simulation is a bit lower than the CSV (\SI{15.90}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{18.50}{\percent}, \(R=\num{0.859}\)).
  \item The \SI{121.78}{keV}, \SI{344.28}{keV}, and especially the \ce{^{60}Co} lines remain noticeably higher in the simulation (\(R=\num{1.749}\) and \num{1.261} for \ce{^{60}Co}).
\end{enumerate}

Some notes on how the pulse clustering affects the \SI{59.54}{keV} \ce{^{241}Am} line, which changed the most when moving from an event-total sum to pulse counting:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item If I simply sum all hits per Geant4 event across the full decay chain, the apparent \SI{59.54}{keV} efficiency in the flush geometry is only \SI{11.41}{\percent}.
  \item However, the \texttt{hits} tree actually stores one row per shower pulse. When I instead merge only rows whose leading-edge times are within \SI{1}{\micro\second} of each other, the efficiency jumps to \SI{28.61}{\percent} for the \SI{1e60}{y} threshold run, and \SI{28.06}{\percent} for the \SI{1e4}{y} cross-check run.
  \item That \num{0.56}-percentage-point difference between the two threshold runs comes from late-chain decays: in the \SI{1e60}{y} file, \num{2204} out of the \num{101387} rows in the \SI{59.54}{keV} window occur at \(t\ge\SI{1e21}{ns}\), meaning they come from \ce{^{237}Np} or its daughters rather than the prompt \ce{^{241}Am} decay. Because those daughter decays are separated in time from the prompt pulse, time clustering keeps them from being summed into the prompt pulse (which is what caused the artificial drop to \SI{11.41}{\percent} in the event sum), though in the \SI{1e60}{y} run they still get counted as separate pulses. So the \SI{1e60}{y} run is really a full-chain stress test, while the \SI{1e4}{y} result (\SI{28.06}{\percent}) is the physical value for a fresh \ce{^{241}Am} source.
\end{enumerate}

Even with pulse clustering and full decay cascades included, the \ce{^{60}Co} lines in the flush simulation remain high compared to the CSV (\SI{0.892}{\percent} and \SI{0.706}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.51}{\percent} and \SI{0.56}{\percent}). Because the flush \ce{^{60}Co} run simulates the full radioactive decay, coincidence summing-out of the \SI{1173.23}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines into the \SI{2505.74}{keV} sum peak is already included (and is quite strong at \(d=\SI{3}{mm}\)). So the takeaway seems to be that while a flush source placement makes the low- and high-energy anchor lines (\SI{46.54}{keV}, \SI{59.54}{keV}, \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{1408.01}{keV}) plausible without tuning any parameters, source distance alone does not resolve the discrepancy on the \ce{^{60}Co} and \ce{^{152}Eu} intermediate lines.

\section{Conclusions}
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item The geometric solid angle sets a strict upper bound $\epsilon\le\epsilon_\Omega(d)$ on the full-energy peak efficiency: \SI{0.0955}{\percent} at \SI{200}{mm}, \SI{0.0428}{\percent} at \SI{300}{mm}, \SI{0.0242}{\percent} at \SI{400}{mm}, and \SI{38.33}{\percent} for the flush-source-to-detector-face geometry ($d=\SI{3}{mm}$).
  \item For \ce{^{137}Cs} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, the simulation/measurement ratio across 200--400 mm is 0.98--1.03 (pulls between $-0.4$ and $+0.8\sigma$).
  \item For \ce{^{60}Co}, the \SI{1332.51}{keV} simulation/measurement ratio is 0.92--1.11 (pulls between $-0.8$ and $+1.8\sigma$), whereas the \SI{1173.23}{keV} ratio is 1.19--1.36 (pulls between $+2.0$ and $+4.4\sigma$). The measured \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} efficiency ratio is 1.00--1.04, compared to 1.18 in the simulation. Since cascade summing is negligible at 200--400 mm, this line-dependent excess likely points to an issue in the Co-60 source geometry or the detector response model.
  \item All measured distance-scan efficiencies come directly from Gaussian-plus-linear fits to the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 spectra (no external efficiency table is used). Comparing the Gaussian fits against local sideband integration gives agreement within about \SI{1}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV}, and a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model systematic at \SI{1173.23}{keV}.
  \item Comparing the \SI{2}{cm} calibration file (\texttt{eff\_clyc1.csv}) against the flush-source simulation ($d_{\mathrm{nom}}=\SI{1}{mm}$, $d=\SI{3}{mm}$) gives reasonable agreement at \SI{46.54}{keV}, \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{1408.01}{keV}, and -- once \SI{1}{\micro\second} pulse clustering is applied -- at \SI{59.54}{keV}. That being said, the \SI{121.78}{keV}, \SI{344.28}{keV}, and \ce{^{60}Co} lines still sit high in the simulation, so an unrecorded flush source position alone does not explain all of the differences in that table.
  \item Fitting the \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} peak widths across 200--400 mm gives a detector resolution model of \(R(E)=(123.9\pm0.4)/\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\%\) (i.e., FWHM \(=1.239\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\mathrm{keV}\)). Smearing the \SI{200}{mm} simulated spectra with this resolution reproduces the measured \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} peaks within about \SI{5}{\percent}, while the \SI{1173.23}{keV} full-energy peak area remains 19--36\% above the measurement.
\end{enumerate}
