\begin{abstract}
Here we compare simulated and measured full-energy peak efficiencies for the CLYC detectors used in the DCS Monitor III project. 
The simulations are broken into a few different campaigns:

\noindent\textbf{Distance variation:} First, we vary the source-to-detector distance between \SI{200}{mm} and \SI{400}{mm}) for the gamma lines from \ce{^{137}Cs} and \ce{^{60}Co}, following the experimental procedure we implemented at HSZG on 27.10.2026. 
The simulations used $10^7$ primary gammas per point using a full-energy peak integration window of $\pm\SI{2}{\percent}$ the gamma line energy. 
The measurements use the data uploaded to the HSZG data share, 2026-10-27 CLYC2, which was converted into ROOT files using a custom tool \texttt{cnf2root}.
These spectra were background-subtracted using the \SI{600}{s} environmental background measurement, followed by a peak-fitting using a Gaussian plus linear background over $\pm1.5$ FWHM. 
At \SI{662}{keV} (\ce{^{137}Cs}) the simulation/measurement ratio is around 0.98--1.03; and at \SI{1332}{keV} (higher-energy line from \ce{^{60}Co}) it is 0.92--1.11. 
However, at \SI{1173}{keV} (lower-energy line from \ce{^{60}Co}) the simulation comes out 19--36\% higher than the measurement. 
Because the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332}{keV} line, the Gaussian peak area has about a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty, however the observed excess is quite a bit larger than that: the measured \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} ratio is 1.00--1.04, compared to 1.18 in the simulation. 
Since cascade summing is negligible at these distances, it seems that this line-dependent excess likely comes from the \ce{^{60}Co} source geometry or the detector response model (the current simulation assumes the source is contained in a \SI{2}{\milli\meter} thick epoxy puck, however a metallic holder could cause more significant attenuation of the \SI{1173}{keV} line, leading to the observed discrepancy).

\noindent\textbf{Flush measurements:} We also compare the CSV efficiency calibration table for CLYC1 (I took a photo of it on 27.10) against a simulation including the full radioactive-decay of a number of sources, as was used to generate the efficiency curve shown on that same printout.
Here, the epoxy puck containing the source was flush with the face of the CLYC detector.
The agreement here is quite good for the following lines: \SI{46.54}{keV}, \SI{59.5}{keV}, \SI{661.64}{keV}, and \SI{1408.01}{keV}.
However, the simulation yields higher efficiencies for the \SI{121.78}{keV}, \SI{344.28}{keV}, and \ce{^{60}Co} lines by over 10 \%.

\noindent\textbf{Spectral shape}
Using the experimental data, a simple model for the detector resolution was determined and implemented in the simulation.
This used data from the distance variation study, i.e., \ce{^{137}Cs} and \ce{^{60}Co}.
The \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines gives a detector energy resolution of $R(E)=(123.9\pm0.4)/\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\%$ (i.e., FWHM $=1.239\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\mathrm{keV}$).
When applied to the simulated data and plotted against the experimental spectra, the agreement is quite close within the photopeak and Compton edge region, however a large deviation exists at low energies, likely due to an absence of back-scattering materials in the G4 model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Geometry and Solid Angle Efficiency Bound}

In the Geant4 model (\texttt{GeometryCLYC.cc}), the detector uses a cylindrical CLYC crystal of diameter \SI{25}{mm} and length \SI{25}{mm} (radius $r=\SI{12.5}{mm}$, density \SI{3.31}{g\,cm^{-3}}) inside a stepped aluminium casing. 
The front face of the crystal is recessed by \SI{2}{mm} behind the outer front surface of the detector casing ($z_{\mathrm{recess}}=\SI{2}{mm}$), with a \SI{0.5}{mm} aluminium entrance window and a \SI{1.5}{mm} Teflon reflector cap in between. 
\textbf{Note: this geometry is as best as I could understand from the blueprints, but could very well be wrong!}
Behind the \SI{29}{mm} snout, the wider detector body and PMT are enclosed by a \SI{0.64}{mm}-thick MuMetal magnetic shield. 
The calibration sources as isotropic point sources sitting at the centre of an epoxy puck of radius \SI{10}{mm}, thickness \SI{2}{mm}, and density \SI{1.16}{g\,cm^{-3}} (\ce{H32C15N2O4}).

For a given nominal source-to-detector distance $d_{\mathrm{nom}}$, the actual distance from the source to the crystal front face is therefore $d=d_{\mathrm{nom}}+\SI{2}{mm}$. \subsection{Solid angle and geometric acceptance}
For an isotropic point source emitting into $4\pi$, the solid angle subtended by the circular crystal entrance face of radius $r$ at distance $d$ along the detector axis is
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(d) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\phi \int_{0}^{\theta_{\max}} \sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta_{\max}) = 2\pi\left(1-\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right),
  \label{eq:solid-angle}
\end{equation}
where $\cos\theta_{\max} = d/\sqrt{d^2+r^2}$, so the corresponding geometric acceptance is
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_\Omega(d)=\frac{\Omega(d)}{4\pi}
  =\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^2+r^2}}\right)\times100\%.
  \label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
For the source distances considered here, this gives:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_\Omega =
  \begin{cases}
    \SI{0.0955}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{200}{mm} \ (d=\SI{202}{mm}),\\
    \SI{0.0428}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{300}{mm} \ (d=\SI{302}{mm}),\\
    \SI{0.0242}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{400}{mm} \ (d=\SI{402}{mm}),\\
    \SI{38.33}{\percent}, & d_{\mathrm{nom}} = \SI{1}{mm} \ (d=\SI{3}{mm})\quad\text{(flush)}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:omega-values}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Maximum efficiency bound and intrinsic efficiency}
We can factorize the total full-energy peak efficiency as
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon=\epsilon_\Omega(d)\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}(E_\gamma,d),
  \label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\in[0,1]$ is the intrinsic efficiency -- i.e., the probability that a gamma entering the crystal deposits all of its energy. Since $\epsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\le1$, the geometric acceptance sets a strict upper bound on the efficiency:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon\le\epsilon_{\max}(d)=\epsilon_\Omega(d).
  \label{eq:eff-max}
\end{equation}

\section{Calculation Methods}

\subsection{Measured efficiency}
Some notes about the experimental data and analysis: the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 spectra were converted from CNF to ROOT using \texttt{cnf2root}, a small converter I built which can extract the channel-count histogram, the calibrated energy histogram, and the acquisition metadata (live time and energy calibration) from the CNF files you uploaded to the cloud (this was easier than installing the GENIE application on a UNIX machine...). 
In the analysis we kept the native $\sim\SI{1.96}{keV}$ energy bins.

First, the background was subtracted using the \SI{600}{s} "Hintergrund" environmental background spectrum. 
This removes room and cosmic background, but does not remove the source-induced Compton continuum. 
We then fitted each full-energy peak with a Gaussian plus linear background over $\pm1.5$ FWHM around the observed peak (i.e., \SI{1113.5}{keV}--\SI{1233.0}{keV} for the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line, and \SI{1264.4}{keV}--\SI{1400.6}{keV} for the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line), taking the Gaussian integral as the measured peak area.

From this, the measured efficiency is given by
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}{A_{\mathrm{exact}}P_\gamma t_{\mathrm{live}}/100}\times100\%,
  \label{eq:eff-exp}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is the source activity on the measurement date, $P_\gamma$ is the gamma-ray emission probability in percent, $t_{\mathrm{live}}=\SI{600}{s}$, and $N_{\mathrm{peak}}$ is the Gaussian peak area (with its statistical uncertainty taken directly from the fit).

For the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line, the peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line. As a cross-check, doing a local sideband integration over the $\pm1$ FWHM peak region (with sidebands at 1.2--1.8 FWHM) gives a peak area that differs from the Gaussian-plus-linear fit by about \SI{10}{\percent}. We treat this \SI{10}{\percent} difference as the background-model systematic for the \SI{1173.23}{keV} line. For the cleaner \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines, the two methods agree to within about \SI{1}{\percent}.

\subsection{Simulated efficiency}
The Geant4 simulations use \texttt{G4EmStandardPhysics\_option4}. For each energy and distance point, we generated $10^7$ monoenergetic gammas isotropically from the centre of the epoxy puck and summed all energy deposited in the CLYC crystal per event. Events are counted in the full-energy peak if they fall within a $\pm2\%$ window:
\begin{equation}
  0.98E_\gamma\le E_{\mathrm{dep}}\le1.02E_\gamma.
  \label{eq:sim-window}
\end{equation}
The simulated efficiency and its statistical uncertainty are then simply
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}{N_{\mathrm{inc}}}\times100\%,
  \qquad
  \sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}=\frac{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{peak}}}}{N_{\mathrm{inc}}}\times100\%,
  \label{eq:eff-sim}
\end{equation}
with $N_{\mathrm{inc}}=10^7$. For this efficiency table we did not apply detector resolution smearing -- in the monoenergetic simulation the full-energy peak is a delta-like line, so the $\pm2\%$ window already captures the entire peak.

To compare simulation and measurement, we use the ratio $R$ and statistical pull:
\begin{equation}
  R=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{pull}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}}
  {\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{exp}}^2}}.
  \label{eq:ratio-pull}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Flush response}
In the flush geometry, the source puck sits directly against the detector front face ($d_{\mathrm{nom}}=\SI{1}{mm}$, with the crystal front face recessed by \SI{2}{mm}, yielding $d=\SI{3}{mm}$ from the source centre to the crystal entrance window). Here we simulated the full radioactive decay of a number of sources (\ce{^{137}Cs}, \ce{^{210}Pb}, \ce{^{241}Am}, \ce{^{152}Eu}, and \ce{^{60}Co}) using \texttt{G4RadioactiveDecayPhysics} with parent ions placed at rest at the puck centre ($N_{\mathrm{decays}}=10^6$ per source), as was done to generate the efficiency calibration curve shown on the HSZG printout.
The simulated peak efficiency is calculated as
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}(E_\gamma)=
  \frac{N_{\mathrm{peak}}(E_\gamma)}{N_{\mathrm{decays}}P_\gamma(E_\gamma)}
  \times100\%,
  \label{eq:flush-eff}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{peak}}(E_\gamma)$ is the number of events with deposited energy falling within the $\pm\SI{2}{\percent}$ window around $E_\gamma$.

The takeaways from the distance scan are:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item At \SI{662}{keV} (\ce{^{137}Cs}), the simulation/measurement ratio is around 0.98--1.03 across the three distances, with pulls between $-0.4$ and $+0.8\sigma$, showing excellent agreement.
  \item At \SI{1332}{keV} (the higher-energy line from \ce{^{60}Co}), the ratio is 0.92--1.11, with pulls between $-0.8$ and $+1.8\sigma$.
  \item However, at \SI{1173}{keV} (the lower-energy line from \ce{^{60}Co}), the simulation comes out 19--36\% higher than the measurement ($R=1.19$--$1.36$), with pulls between $+2.0$ and $+4.4\sigma$.
\end{enumerate}

The \SI{1173.23}{keV} line is clearly the outlier here. Because the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332}{keV} line, the Gaussian peak area has about a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty. However, the observed excess is quite a bit larger than that: the measured \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} ratio is 1.00--1.04, compared to 1.18 in the simulation. And because the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line agrees within 0.92--1.11, this is a line-dependent difference rather than a simple overall normalization offset.

Cascade summing is negligible at these distances: at \SI{200}{mm}--\SI{400}{mm}, the total interaction probability per gamma in the crystal is only about $3.3\times10^{-4}$, so the probability for both cascade gammas to interact in the same decay is around $10^{-7}$ and the \SI{2505.74}{keV} sum peak has fewer than 5 expected counts. Since cascade summing is negligible at these distances, it seems that this line-dependent excess likely comes from the \ce{^{60}Co} source geometry or the detector response model (the current simulation assumes the source is contained in a \SI{2}{\milli\meter} thick epoxy puck).

\section{Experimental Spectra and Peak Integration}
\label{sec:peak_integration}

As noted above, the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 runs are the only measured spectra used in this report. Following the CNF-to-ROOT conversion and \SI{600}{s} environmental background subtraction, the $\pm1.5$ FWHM Gaussian-plus-linear fits give the measured efficiencies listed in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} (for example, the \SI{1173.23}{keV} fit window is \SI{1113.5}{keV}--\SI{1233.0}{keV}). Comparing those Gaussian areas against local sideband integration over the same peak region gives agreement to within about \SI{1}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV}, and about a \SI{10}{\percent} difference at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, which we use as the continuum-model systematic.

To look a bit more closely at the background shape under the \ce{^{60}Co} lines, Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co_fits} shows diagnostic Gaussian-plus-quadratic-background fits to the \SI{200}{mm} \ce{^{60}Co} spectra. While Table~\ref{tab:comparison} uses the linear background model for consistency across all runs, using a quadratic background here accounts a bit better for the curved Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line sitting underneath the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak. With the quadratic background, the fitted simulation/experiment peak-area ratios are 1.21 at \SI{1173.23}{keV} and 1.04 at \SI{1332.51}{keV} (compared to 1.30 and 1.11 in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}); the shift between the two fits reflects the background-model uncertainty on the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak.

\section{Spectral Shape and Resolution Model}
\label{sec:resolution}

\subsection{Resolution model}
Using the experimental data, a simple model for the detector resolution was determined and implemented in the simulation.
This used data from the distance variation study, i.e., \ce{^{137}Cs} and \ce{^{60}Co}.
From the Gaussian-plus-linear fits at \SI{200}{mm}, \SI{300}{mm}, and \SI{400}{mm}, we extracted the FWHM of the \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{1332.51}{keV} peaks. The widths are quite consistent across all three distances; Table~\ref{tab:resolution} lists the mean values and the standard error of the mean. This gives an energy resolution \(R=\mathrm{FWHM}/E\) of \SI{4.80}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{3.40}{\percent} at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{3.41}{\percent} at \SI{1332.51}{keV}.

Because the \SI{1173.23}{keV} peak sits on top of the Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line, its fitted width is biased a bit by the underlying continuum. We therefore excluded it from the resolution fit and fitted only the two cleaner lines (\SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV}). The \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines give a detector energy resolution of
\begin{equation}
  R(E)=\frac{123.9\pm0.4}{\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}}\,\%
  \qquad
  \text{(i.e., FWHM $=1.239\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\mathrm{keV}$)}.
  \label{eq:res-model}
\end{equation}
The \SI{1173.23}{keV} point sits \SI{6.2}{\percent} below this curve, consistent with background-model bias under the peak.

This resolution model was then implemented in the simulation to smear the deposited energy spectra for direct visual and spectral comparison with experiment: for each event with deposited energy \(E_{\mathrm{dep}}\), the smeared energy is sampled from a Gaussian with mean \(E_{\mathrm{dep}}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma(E_{\mathrm{dep}})=\mathrm{FWHM}(E_{\mathrm{dep}})/2.3548\). This smearing conserves event counts and does not alter the integrated full-energy peak areas.

\subsection{Comparison of simulated and experimental spectra at 200 mm}
\label{sec:spectra_200mm}

Now to the plots comparing the full spectra at \SI{200}{mm}. For these, we ran $10^7$-event monoenergetic simulations with the GPS emission restricted to a \SI{15}{\degree} half-angle cone around the detector axis to get better statistics in the crystal. Restricting the emission to the cone boosts the number of gammas reaching the crystal by the inverse of the cone solid-angle fraction, $f=(1-\cos15^\circ)/2=0.017037$, corresponding to an effective isotropic incident count of $N_{\mathrm{inc,eff}}=N_{\mathrm{gen}}/f=\num{5.87e8}$. As a sanity check, the cone runs reproduce the isotropic full-energy peak efficiencies within about \(1\sigma\): \SI{0.00871}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00843}{\percent} at \SI{661.64}{keV}, \SI{0.00373}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00384}{\percent} at \SI{1173.23}{keV}, and \SI{0.00308}{\percent} vs.\ \SI{0.00326}{\percent} at \SI{1332.51}{keV}. Since the \SI{15}{\degree} cone cut drops only wide-angle gammas that would have scattered into the crystal from outside the cone (which contribute mainly to the continuum), the full-energy peak comparison remains quantitative while the low-energy continuum comparison is qualitative.

Both the smeared and unsmeared simulated spectra use the exact same events and scaling. With the expected number of emitted gammas given by
\[
  N_{\mathrm{emit}}=A_{\mathrm{exact}}P_\gamma t_{\mathrm{live}}/100,
\]
the histogram scale factor is $N_{\mathrm{emit}}f/N_{\mathrm{gen}}$. For \ce{^{137}Cs} at \SI{200}{mm}, $N_{\mathrm{emit}}=\num{2.834e8}$ and the scale factor is 0.4828. For \ce{^{60}Co}, we summed the \SI{1173.23}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} runs, scaling each by its respective $N_{\mathrm{emit}}$ (\num{7.031e8} and \num{7.032e8}, scale factor 1.198). Since the two lines are generated in separate monoenergetic runs, the \SI{2505.74}{keV} coincidence sum peak is not present in the simulation.

When applied to the simulated data and plotted against the experimental spectra, the agreement is quite close within the photopeak and Compton edge region, however a large deviation exists at low energies, likely due to an absence of back-scattering materials in the G4 model.

Looking at each source in detail:
For \ce{^{137}Cs} at \SI{200}{mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_cs}), the resolution-smeared simulation matches the \SI{661.64}{keV} photopeak and Compton edge quite well: in the \(\pm2\%\) energy window, the ratio of background-subtracted data counts to the smeared simulation is 0.955, and the smeared simulated peak area is only about \SI{5}{\percent} above the measured Gaussian area. At lower energies, however, the simulation sits substantially below the measured continuum. This large deviation at low energies is likely due to an absence of back-scattering materials in the G4 model (such as the optical table, source holder, detector stand, walls, and surrounding experimental structures). Also, the measured \SI{200}{mm} \ce{^{137}Cs} spectrum does not show a resolved \SI{32}{keV} Ba X-ray peak, likely because the source encapsulation absorbs those low-energy X-rays, whereas the monoenergetic simulation only generates the \SI{661.64}{keV} gamma anyway.

For \ce{^{60}Co} at \SI{200}{mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co}), Table~\ref{tab:comparison} gave $\epsilon_{\mathrm{sim}}/\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}=1.30$ at \SI{1173.23}{keV} and 1.11 at \SI{1332.51}{keV}. Note that the bin-by-bin ratio plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co}(b) looks a bit different near \SI{1173.23}{keV} because it is an interval ratio rather than a background-subtracted peak-area ratio: the \(\pm2\%\) window at \SI{1173.23}{keV} is \SI{46.9}{keV} wide (\num{1.18} FWHM) and includes Compton continuum from the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line. Specifically, the background-subtracted data integral in that window is \num{23396} counts (vs.\ \num{20800} in the Gaussian peak), whereas the smeared simulation has \num{24306} counts in the window (and about \num{26200} in the full-energy peak). Taking the raw window ratio gives \num{0.963}, whereas comparing the actual full-energy peak counts gives $20{,}819/26{,}210=0.79$ (i.e., a simulation/measurement ratio of \num{1.26}, consistent with the 1.30 in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).

Figure~\ref{fig:exp_sim_co_fits} shows the explicit peak-plus-background decompositions, where the fitted simulation/experiment peak ratios are 1.21 and 1.04. Even with the \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty from Sec.~\ref{sec:peak_integration}, the \SI{1173.23}{keV} simulation still sits high, while the \SI{1332.51}{keV} line agrees within the peak-integration uncertainty. Again, the photopeak and Compton edge show close shape agreement, while at low energies the simulation underestimates the continuum due to the missing back-scattering materials. A couple of other features in the measured \ce{^{60}Co} spectrum are worth noting: weak background or impurity lines at \SI{141.8}{keV} and \SI{222.1}{keV} are present in the experiment and absent in the simulation, and the \SI{2505.74}{keV} sum peak is absent in the monoenergetic simulation. However, as noted before, cascade summing is negligible at \SI{200}{mm} (summing-out probability below \SI{0.05}{\percent}), so cascade summing cannot explain the \SI{1173.23}{keV} excess.

\section{Conclusions}
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Distance variation (\ce{^{137}Cs} and \ce{^{60}Co}, 200--400 mm):} Across the distance scan, the simulation reproduces the measured full-energy peak efficiency at \SI{662}{keV} (\ce{^{137}Cs}) with a simulation/measurement ratio of 0.98--1.03 (pulls $-0.4$ to $+0.8\sigma$), and at \SI{1332}{keV} (\ce{^{60}Co}) with 0.92--1.11 (pulls $-0.8$ to $+1.8\sigma$). However, at \SI{1173}{keV} (\ce{^{60}Co}) the simulation comes out 19--36\% higher than the measurement ($R=1.19$--$1.36$, pulls $+2.0$ to $+4.4\sigma$).
  \item \textbf{Line-dependent \ce{^{60}Co} discrepancy:} The measured \SI{1173}{keV}/\SI{1332}{keV} peak efficiency ratio is 1.00--1.04, compared to 1.18 in the simulation. Although the \SI{1173}{keV} Gaussian peak area has about a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model uncertainty from the underlying \SI{1332}{keV} Compton continuum, the observed excess exceeds this systematic. Because cascade summing is negligible at 200--400 mm (summing probability $\sim10^{-7}$), this line-dependent excess likely points to the \ce{^{60}Co} source geometry or the detector response model.
  \item \textbf{Experimental peak integration:} All distance-scan efficiencies were obtained directly from Gaussian-plus-linear fits to the 2026-10-27 CLYC2 spectra after subtracting the \SI{600}{s} environmental background. Cross-checking against local sideband integration shows agreement within \SI{1}{\percent} for the isolated \SI{662}{keV} and \SI{1332}{keV} lines, and confirms a \SI{10}{\percent} background-model systematic for the \SI{1173}{keV} line.
  \item \textbf{Flush response:} Comparing the CSV efficiency calibration table for CLYC1 against the full radioactive-decay simulation with the epoxy puck flush with the detector face yields good agreement for \SI{46.54}{keV} ($R=1.058$), \SI{59.54}{keV} ($R=1.202$), \SI{661.64}{keV} ($R=1.100$), and \SI{1408.01}{keV} ($R=1.079$). However, the simulation yields higher efficiencies for \SI{121.78}{keV} ($+17\%$), \SI{344.28}{keV} ($+29\%$), and the \ce{^{60}Co} lines ($+75\%$ and $+26\%$). Thus, while the flush geometry correctly matches the low- and high-energy anchor points, source distance alone does not resolve the intermediate-line discrepancies.
  \item \textbf{Detector resolution and spectral shape:} Using the \SI{661.64}{keV} and \SI{1332.51}{keV} lines from the distance scan, we determined a detector energy resolution model of $R(E)=(123.9\pm0.4)/\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\%$ (FWHM $=1.239\sqrt{E/\mathrm{keV}}\,\mathrm{keV}$). When applied to the simulated data, the resolution-smeared spectra match the experimental photopeaks and Compton edges quite well. At low energies, a large deviation exists where the simulation underestimates the measured continuum, likely due to an absence of back-scattering materials in the Geant4 model.
\end{enumerate}
