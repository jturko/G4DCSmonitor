\author{J.~Turko}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We describe a \gfour\ (v11.3.2) simulation of a
\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{10}{\milli\meter} polished
plastic scintillator (PVT, UPS-92S/EJ-200 class) read out by side-coupled
silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs), modelled on the muon-trigger detector of
Rao \emph{et al.}\ [J.~Appl.\ Phys.\ \textbf{138}, 114504 (2025)], and ask how
far it reproduces the \emph{measured} light-collection behaviour of four
surface treatments.

The physics question is sharper than it first appears, because the four
treatments differ in \emph{how} they return light to the plate: the paint
returns it diffusely, the two mirrors specularly, and the bare plate returns
none at all. A polished plate facing a black holder traps light by total
internal reflection over a cone of half-angle \SI{39.3}{\degree}, but every
photon outside that cone dies on the black wall, whereas the two mirrors keep
the same cone and hand the escaped fraction back with a reflectivity of
\num{0.90}--\num{0.96}. Even in the idealised model the bare plate is
therefore dimmer than both mirrors---only the diffuse paint is dimmer---and
in the measurement it is the dimmest of the four. We resolve that inversion
with two calibrated surface
parameters---a mean-field plate--holder contact fraction $f$ and the optical
backing of the aluminium foil---and then show that the measured ordering is
recovered for every directly-coupled layout.

The five layouts include a corner-cut variant, Config~2 (mod), which replaces
the square corners by \SI{45}{\degree} chamfers and mounts one sensor on each
cut face. With $f=\num{0.045}$ and an air-backed foil, a \num{60}-file matrix
scan (5 SiPM layouts $\times$ 4 treatments $\times$ 3 beam positions,
\num{2500} events each) and a whole-plate irradiation (\num{25000} events per
configuration and treatment) give, for all five layouts,
\[
  \text{bare} < \text{TiO}_2 < \text{Al} < \text{glossy},
\]
with glossy paper brightest, air-backed aluminium within
\SIrange{91}{92}{\percent} of it, and the contact-limited bare plate weakest.
Config~2 (mod) keeps the ordering but moves the brightest patch to the
instrumented corner and leaves the plate centre relatively dark. The
ordering is explained by a single mechanism:
the response tracks the \emph{distance from the interaction point to the
nearest sensor} whenever the cavity is not a perfect light mixer, so the
diffuse paint (contrast $\kappa_{\rm 2D}=\num{1.91}$--\num{10.90}) and the
contact-limited bare plate ($\num{1.56}$--$\num{4.14}$) are position
dependent, while the specular wrappers ($\num{1.07}$--\num{1.37}) are not.
That same mechanism is what makes the eight channels of config~1 a position
detector: using a symmetry-augmented nearest-neighbour regression that
combines the integrated charges with a \SI{12.5}{\percent}
optical constant-fraction (CFD) wavefront timing observable, the diffuse paint
localises the muon to \SI{12.3}{\milli\meter} per axis from light sharing alone
(\SI{11.9}{\milli\meter} combined), whereas the specular wrappers carry the
information in the arrival-time wavefront instead and reach
\SI{15.4}{\milli\meter}--\SI{16.2}{\milli\meter} per axis (\SI{19.4}{\milli\meter}
for the bare plate). We also report per-event photon
yields, photoelectron spectra, wavelength and arrival-time distributions, and
a systematic comparison with the published measurements, including the one
wrapper for which the simulation and the measurement disagree on the sign of
the position trend. A ten-design controlled campaign then isolates what each
design knob is worth: the larger sensor is the only near-total improvement
(\num{2.1} times the light and \num{1.4} times better position resolution
for \num{2.25} times its area), blackening the four narrow faces buys a
factor \num{3.6} in timing at \SI{88}{\percent} of the light, and a fibre
read-out on every face buys the flattest response and the best resolution per
photoelectron at the price of speed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and motivation}
% =====================================================================

Plastic scintillators read out at their edges by SiPMs are attractive for
large-area, low-cost muon triggers and veto systems for cosmic-ray muon
tomography~\cite{Borozdin2003,Bonechi2020}. The amount of scintillation
light that reaches the photosensor is governed almost entirely by how the
non-instrumented faces of the plate are treated: a polished face traps light
by total internal reflection (TIR) over a cone of half-angle
\SI{39.3}{\degree}, and what happens to the light that leaves that
cone---handed back by a mirror, re-emitted diffusely by a paint, or absorbed
by a black wall---decides the yield. Understanding this trade-off
quantitatively is the central design question, and it is precisely where
simulation is most useful, because the photon transport is a multi-bounce
random walk that is difficult to model analytically~\cite{Korpachev2017}.

Of the four surface treatments compared in
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}---no reflective layer at all, a white TiO$_2$ paint,
aluminium foil and high-gloss paper---the bare plate is the only one that
returns no light to the plate at all. It is a polished TIR guide, but every
photon outside its \SI{39.3}{\degree} cone crosses the air gap and is
absorbed by the black holder, whereas the two mirrors keep exactly the same
cone and hand the escaped fraction back with a reflectivity of
\num{0.90}--\num{0.96}. The naive expectation, that a polished plate in a
black holder should be at least as good as a diffuse paint, is the
opposite of what the experiment finds, and the reason is not obvious: in the
idealised model ($f=0$) the bare plate collects \num{305}~\pe/event at the
config-1 centre---more than the diffuse paint (\num{125}) but less than
air-backed aluminium (\num{482}) and glossy paper (\num{521})---whereas in the
measurement it collects the \emph{least} of the four. A clamped plate touches
its holder at scattered high spots, and each contact frustrates the total
internal reflection that the whole design relies on. Reproducing the
measurement therefore requires modelling not only the reflectors but also
the \emph{non}-reflector, and the size of that hidden loss channel has to be
calibrated against data.

This report documents a \gfour\ (v11.3.2)~\cite{Agostinelli2003,Allison2016}
simulation built to address that question for the specific geometry and
surface treatments of Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. The deliverable is a
self-contained ``matrix scan'': every combination of five SiPM layouts and
four reflector treatments, at three beam positions on the plate, plus a
uniform whole-plate irradiation. The goal is not to fit a single number but
to test how far the simulation reproduces the \emph{ordering} and the
\emph{position dependence} of the light-collection efficiency that the
experiment observes, to expose the physics behind both the parts it
reproduces and the parts it does not, and to quantify the residuals.

\section{The measurement being reproduced}
\label{sec:measurement}
% =====================================================================

Before presenting results it is worth stating what is being reproduced, in
the terms of the experiment, and where the model and the experiment are
known to differ. Everything in this section is quoted from
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}.

\subsection{What was measured}

A \SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{10}{\milli\meter}
plate of UPS-92S organic scintillator is read out by
CEFLA S.C.\ ASD-NUV4S-P-40 SiPMs, each with a \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter}
active area, mounted on the \emph{narrow} side faces of the plate. That
choice is deliberate: because the area of the narrow faces is small, a large
fraction of the light is transported by total internal reflection off the
large faces and then collected towards the narrow ones, so the detected yield
is higher on the narrow faces than on the large ones. Configurations with
the SiPM and the fibre clamp on the large faces were explicitly not
considered, for the same reason.

Four \emph{surface treatments} were applied to the non-instrumented faces,
in addition to the bare plate:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item EJ-510 white reflector paint with TiO$_2$ suspension (Eljen
        Technology);
  \item commercial aluminium foil, shiny side out, with small amounts of
        optical grease applied selectively to ensure contact between the
        scintillator and the reflective layer;
  \item no reflective material at all --- the polished plate simply
        \emph{placed} in a black 3D-printed holder;
  \item super high-gloss resin-coated photo paper (\SI{300}{\gram\per\square\meter},
        Print4Life), cut to size and placed on each surface \emph{without}
        grease.
\end{enumerate}
These are the four treatments modelled here, one-to-one.

Four \emph{read-out configurations} were then assembled (Table~IV of
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}); these are modelled here, and a fifth variant is added:
\begin{description}
  \item[Config 1] eight SiPMs, two on each of the four narrow faces
        (\texttt{TBLR1}, \texttt{TBLR2}), \SI{128}{\milli\meter\squared} of
        active area in total;
  \item[Config 2] four SiPMs, one on each narrow face (\texttt{TBLR1}),
        \SI{64}{\milli\meter\squared};
  \item[Config 2 (mod)] the same four \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter}
        sensors and \SI{64}{\milli\meter\squared} of active area as Config~2,
        but mounted on four \SI{45}{\degree} corner chamfers instead of the
        lateral faces; the plate becomes a convex octagon (Sec.~\ref{sec:detector});
  \item[Config 3] two SiPMs, both on the same narrow face (\texttt{T1},
        \texttt{T2}), \SI{32}{\milli\meter\squared};
  \item[Config 4] one SiPM plus a wavelength-shifting fibre clamp, whose
        four fibres provide an effective collection area of
        \SI{2500}{\milli\meter\squared}.
\end{description}

The efficiency was measured with a muon coincidence detector (MCD) system of
two \SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{10}{\milli\meter}
reflective-painted reference scintillators read out by photomultiplier
tubes. The MCD was placed over one of three \emph{regions of interest} on
the plate --- the central region, a middle-edge region and a corner region
--- and coincidences between the two MCD arms provided the reference muon
sample. The reported observable is the detection efficiency
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DE}(u_{\mathrm{thresh}})
  = \frac{N_{\mathrm{det}}(u_{\mathrm{thresh}})}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}},
  \label{eq:de}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the fraction of reference muons that produce a trigger signal above the
discriminator threshold $u_{\mathrm{thresh}}$, together with the pulse-height
(detected photon yield) distribution of the trigger itself.

\subsection{The statements this simulation is tested against}

The experiment reports a small number of clear, qualitative statements, and
these are the yardstick used in Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item configurations \emph{without} a reflective layer consistently
        produce the lowest detected photon yield at the SiPMs;
  \item the signal-to-noise ratios are highest and nearly identical for the
        aluminium foil and the high-gloss paper;
  \item the lowest detected photon yield distributions occur for all
        configurations when the MCD is positioned at the \emph{corner} of
        the scintillator plate;
  \item all configurations except those without a reflective layer reach a
        detection efficiency of \SI{80}{\percent} or more at
        $u_{\mathrm{thresh}} = \SI{400}{\milli\volt}$, whereas the bare
        configuration is the only one that falls short;
  \item the WLS-fibre configuration (config~4) produces a significantly
        higher detected photon yield distribution than config~3 at all
        measurement positions, and reaches a detection efficiency of
        \SI{90}{\percent} at $u_{\mathrm{thresh}} = \SI{400}{\milli\volt}$
        across its full active area;
  \item the converted (wavelength-shifted) photons still produce electrical
        signals with approximately \SI{30}{\percent} efficiency.
\end{itemize}
The first three concern the \emph{ordering} of the treatments and the
\emph{sign} of the position trend, and are the statements this simulation
reproduces, fails to reproduce, or contradicts; the fourth and fifth are
threshold statements that the simulation can address only indirectly, since
it contains no electronics (Sec.~\ref{sec:caveats}); the sixth quantifies
the optical efficiency of the WLS path and is the natural yardstick for the
one configuration that the simulation does \emph{not} reproduce.

\subsection{Where the model deliberately departs from the experiment}

Two geometric departures are worth stating explicitly, because both affect
the comparison and neither is accidental.

\emph{The config-3 and config-4 faces.} In the experiment, config~3 reads out
the \emph{top} narrow face and the config-4 fibre clamp sits on the
\emph{left} narrow face --- two adjacent, not shared, faces. In this
simulation both assemblies are placed on the same narrow face of the model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:detector}), which has the advantage of keeping the four
layouts on a common geometric footing and of making the two single-sided
layouts directly comparable to each other, at the cost of removing the
adjacency that the experiment realises. Any statement about the relative
performance of config~3 and config~4 in this report therefore refers to the
shared-face geometry and cannot be read as a statement about the
experimental pair.

\emph{The position of the irradiated patches.} The three regions of interest
are defined by where the MCD is placed, and in the experiment they are
chosen so that the MCD (which is \SI{50}{\milli\meter} square) sits entirely
on the plate. Here the same requirement is imposed, and the edge and corner
patches are placed on the side of the plate \emph{opposite} the instrumented
face of the single-sided layouts, so that the three patches sample the far
side of the cavity for configs~3 and~4.

Finally, the experiment itself warns that the four configurations
``cannot be directly compared with one another'', because the number of
parallel SiPMs changes the total input capacitance, the effective gain and
the mean probability of a photon hitting the sensitive area. The same
caveat applies here, and it is the reason the results below are presented
per layout rather than pooled.

\section{The detector and its optical model}
\label{sec:detector}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Scintillator plate}

The active volume is a rectangular PVT scintillator
$250 \times 250 \times \SI{10}{\milli\meter}$, mirroring the UPS-92S plate of
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. It is assigned the \gfour\ NIST base material
\texttt{G4\_PLASTIC\_SC\_VINYLTOLUENE} with a custom optical properties table
built from the EJ-200 and UPS-923A data
sheets~\cite{EljenEJ200,Artikov2005,AmcrysPS}. The optical constants---the
refractive index ($n = 1.58$, essentially flat across the visible), light yield,
emission peak, bulk attenuation length, scintillation decay and rise times, and
the Birks constant~\cite{Birks1951,Craun1970}---are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:detparams} and are not repeated here. Two entries are
deliberate modelling choices rather than quoted numbers: the bulk attenuation
length is peaked at \SI{3.8}{\meter} but falls steeply in the UV to model
self-absorption of the short-wavelength part of the emission, and the emission
spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra_opt}) is essentially zero below
\SI{380}{\nano\meter} and above \SI{550}{\nano\meter}. Both scintillation
and \v{C}erenkov photons are produced and tracked; the optical grid spans
\SIrange{310}{700}{\nano\meter} so that the \v{C}erenkov continuum, which
extends well below the scintillation band, is covered.

For Config 2 (mod) only, the plate is a convex octagon rather than a box: each
corner is cut by a \SI{45}{\degree} plane with a \SI{12}{\milli\meter} leg, so
the outline is $\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert \le \SI{238}{\milli\meter}$ and
the eight vertices are $(125,\pm113)$, $(-125,\pm113)$, $(\pm113,125)$ and
$(\pm113,-125)\,\si{\milli\meter}$. The solid is built
analytically as a \gfour\ \texttt{G4ExtrudedSolid}, so the four diagonal faces
have exact normals; the four \SI{4}{\milli\meter} sensors are centred on them at
$(\pm119,\pm119)\,\si{\milli\meter}$ (report frame) and are flush with the
face. The cut removes \SI{0.461}{\percent} of the plate area, all at the
corners. Setting the cut length to zero restores the plain \texttt{G4Box}
bit-for-bit, which is how the other layouts are built.

\subsection{SiPM readout and coupling}

Each SiPM~\cite{Gundacker2020} is modelled mechanically as a
$4 \times 4 \times \SI{0.6}{\milli\meter}$ box of ``SiPM epoxy'' ($n = 1.55$),
coupled to a side face of the plate through a
\SI{0.1}{\milli\meter} silicone grease pad ($n = 1.46$). The coupling chain is
therefore \emph{scintillator\,$\to$\,grease\,$\to$\,SiPM}, with Fresnel
transmission at each dielectric interface and photodetection at the final
grease/SiPM boundary. This describes the side-mounted sensors of
configs~1, 2, 2 (mod) and~3.
In config~4 the read-out SiPM instead sits at the $-y$ end of the WLS fibre
bundle, and its \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter} grease pad
overlaps the slab's $-y$ face: the pad centre is at
$(x,y)=(-125.55,-125.05)\,\si{\milli\meter}$, so \SI{1.45}{\milli\meter} of its
width crosses the slab edge and \SI{5.8}{\milli\meter\squared} of the
\SI{16}{\milli\meter\squared} pad is in direct optical contact with the
scintillator. That contact is modelled explicitly and is on by default
(Sec.~\ref{sec:wls}), so the config-4 SiPM receives both direct blue light
through the contact and green wavelength-shifted light from the fibre ends.

Five layouts are studied (schematic in Fig.~\ref{fig:layouts}):
\begin{description}
  \item[Config 1 (8 SiPMs)] two SiPMs per side face, at $\pm 50\%$ of the half-length.
  \item[Config 2 (4 SiPMs)] one SiPM per side face, at the \emph{same} $\pm 50\%$
        offsets as config~1---i.e.\ config~1 with one device removed per side,
        the removed slot rotated from side to side so the layout remains
        four-fold symmetric.
  \item[Config 2 (mod) (4 SiPMs)] the four corners of the square are truncated
        by \SI{45}{\degree} cuts with a \SI{12}{\milli\meter} leg, giving a convex
        octagon with four \SI{17.0}{\milli\meter}-wide diagonal faces at
        $(\pm119,\pm119)\,\si{\milli\meter}$ in the report frame. One
        \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter} SiPM is centred on each
        cut face, so the four sensor normals point towards the plate centre.
        This is a variant of Config~2, not one of the four Table~IV layouts.
  \item[Config 3 (2 SiPMs)] both SiPMs on the \emph{same} narrow
        face---the $-x$ face in the simulation frame, which is the report
        plan view's top edge (Sec.~\ref{sec:sim})---at $\pm 50\%$, matching
        T1/T2 of Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}.
  \item[Config 4 (1 SiPM $+$ WLS clamp)] a single SiPM reading out a
        wavelength-shifting (WLS) fibre clamp along the $-x$ narrow face
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:wls}).
\end{description}
SiPM footprints are clamped so they cannot overhang the slab face.

\subsection{Reflector treatments}
\label{sec:reflectors}

Four treatments are applied as a \emph{logical skin} over the non-instrumented
surfaces of the plate; the grease/SiPM patches override the skin locally with
dedicated \emph{border} surfaces. The mapping from each physical treatment to the
\gfour\ optical-surface parameters is the heart of the model---it determines
the light-collection ordering---so it is given in full. Table~\ref{tab:surfaces}
lists every property that is actually attached to each surface, and the
subsubsections below describe each reflector physically, state how it is
modelled, and assess how faithfully the model represents the real object. (A
fifth finish, PTFE tape, exists in the code for completeness but is not part of
the reference study.) The strong sensitivity of light collection to the wrapping
and reflector choice is well established
experimentally~\cite{Taheri2017}.

\subsubsection{Bare plate in its black holder}
\label{sec:refl-bare}

\emph{Physical object.} The polished UPS-92S plate is placed in a black
3D-printed ABS enclosure, its faces simply resting near the wall.

\emph{Model.} No wrapping skin is attached. The plate keeps its native polished
scintillator ($n=1.58$) against air ($n=1.0003$) across a real
\SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} gap; light below the critical angle
($\arcsin(1/1.58)=\SI{39.3}{\degree}$) refracts across the gap and is absorbed in
the holder, whose black ABS has an attenuation length of
\SI{1}{\micro\meter}, while light above it is returned by total internal
reflection. Fresnel reflection and TIR are taken from the material
\texttt{RINDEX} tables.

\emph{Fidelity.} This is the least faithful configuration and the source of the
report's central modelling choice. A real clamped plate touches the holder at
scattered high spots, and each contact forms a frustrated-TIR sink that removes
trapped light; surface scratches do the same. Those loss channels are absent in
the clean air-gap model ($f=0$), where the plate keeps the full TIR cone but
loses the whole sub-critical fraction at the black wall: it is brighter than
the diffuse paint but dimmer than the two mirrors in every layout
(\num{305}~\pe/event at the config-1 centre, against \num{482} for air-backed
aluminium and \num{521} for glossy paper), whereas the reference
measurement gives it the \emph{lowest} signal of all four treatments. The code
therefore offers an optional mean-field contact fraction,
\texttt{/dcs-monitor/det/muonScint/setBareContactFraction <f>}
($0\le f\le 1$): for $f>0$ every hit on a bare face is absorbed with probability
$f$ (a polished \texttt{dielectric\_dielectric} skin with
\texttt{REFLECTIVITY} $=1-f$ and \texttt{TRANSMITTANCE} $=0$), representing
frustrated TIR over an effective contact area. The loss is applied to every
bare layout, including config~4, whose bare faces carry the same skin (only the
clamp face is exempt, being coupled to the plate through the grease film). The
dedicated scan
(Sec.~\ref{sec:contact}) gives a nominal
$f=\num{0.045}$, the smallest sampled fraction that places all nine
config/position cells below the corresponding TiO$_2$ reference; $f=0$ is kept
as the clean-air reference and lower bound on the loss.

\subsubsection{Titanium-dioxide paint (EJ-510)}
\label{sec:refl-tio2}

\emph{Physical object.} A white, diffuse-reflectance paint applied directly to
the plate (three coats, $\approx\SI{0.11}{\milli\meter}$), in optical contact
with the scintillator.

\emph{Model.} \texttt{groundfrontpainted} with a purely Lambertian reflection: a
random draw against \texttt{REFLECTIVITY} returns the photon with a cosine-law
distribution and \emph{no} specular or TIR component
($f_{ss}=f_{sl}=f_{bs}=0$). The reflectance rises from \num{0.955} in the UV to
a plateau of \num{0.983} near \SI{470}{\nano\meter}.

\emph{Fidelity.} The Lambertian/no-TIR treatment is the physically correct
description of light emerging from a thick, strongly scattering paint, and is
this wrapper's strongest feature. The \emph{absolute} reflectance curve is its
weakest: no public spectral-reflectance data exist for EJ-510, so $R(\lambda)$
is a fitted representative white-paint curve ($\approx 0.96$--$0.98$), not a
measurement of the actual coating, and no angular dependence of the diffuse
lobe is modelled. The \texttt{sigma\_alpha} value is set for bookkeeping but is
inert at this finish (\texttt{groundfrontpainted} reflects diffusely regardless
of the facet normal).

\subsubsection{Aluminium foil}
\label{sec:refl-al}

\emph{Physical object.} Commercial shiny aluminium foil wrapped around the
plate; per Ref.~\cite{Rao2025} optical grease is applied in places so that the
foil contacts the scintillator rather than leaving an air gap.

\emph{Model.} \texttt{polishedbackpainted} with a selectable backing refractive
index, chosen pre-init with
\texttt{/dcs-monitor/det/muonScint/setAlBacking air|grease}. The production
choice is \texttt{air} ($n=1.0003$), which leaves the full TIR cone intact and
lets only the sub-critical fraction cross to the foil, modelling a foil
separated from the plate by a trapped air layer. The alternative,
\texttt{grease} ($n=1.46$), represents a foil applied over the coupling grease,
as in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}; the scintillator/grease interface is a normal
Fresnel/TIR boundary, but since the index step is small the critical angle is
wide ($\arcsin(1.46/1.58)=\SI{67.5}{\degree}$): most light refracts into the
grease film and is returned by the foil as a pure \emph{specular} reflection
($f_{ss}=1$). It is retained as the lower-bound systematic. In both cases
\texttt{REFLECTIVITY} is the normal-incidence reflectance of evaporated
aluminium computed from the McPeak \emph{et al.}~$n,k$
data~\cite{McPeak2015} (\num{0.904} at \SI{650}{\nano\meter} rising to
\num{0.925} at \SI{360}{\nano\meter}).

\emph{Fidelity.} Two idealisations. (i) The tabulated reflectance is that of a
smooth evaporated film and is therefore an \emph{upper bound}: household foil is
rougher and carries a native oxide, both of which lower $R$ and add a small
diffuse component we do not model. (ii) The foil is assumed to cover the plate
uniformly, whereas real wrapping leaves wrinkles and trapped air. The production
campaign uses the \texttt{air} backing, which retains the full TIR cone and
recovers the measured near-equality of aluminium and glossy paper, reaching
\SIrange{91}{92}{\percent} of the glossy yield in the five direct-coupled
layouts.
The \texttt{grease} backing is retained as the lower-bound systematic
(Sec.~\ref{sec:albacking}). Note that the air backing is itself the
\emph{optimistic} end of the physical range: it is the limiting case of a
perfectly trapped air layer, whereas the experiment describes grease applied
\emph{selectively}, i.e.\ a foil that contacts the plate in places and is
decoupled in others. Neither extreme is the real object, and the scan of
Sec.~\ref{sec:albacking} brackets them.

\subsubsection{High-gloss photo paper}
\label{sec:refl-glossy}

\emph{Physical object.} A commercial super-high-gloss photographic paper
(Print4Life, \SI{300}{\gram\per\square\meter}) wrapped around the plate with an
air gap (no grease).

\emph{Model.} \texttt{polishedbackpainted} with the backing index set to air
($n=1.0003$), so the plate retains its full TIR cone and only the sub-critical
fraction crosses the gap to the paper, which returns it as a specular reflection
($f_{ss}=1$) with a fitted $R(\lambda)\approx 0.900$--$0.955$---slightly lossier
than the aluminium.

\emph{Fidelity.} The reference reports glossy paper and aluminium foil as the two
best wrappers (large, nearly equal signals), which motivates modelling glossy
paper as a \emph{slightly lossy mirror} rather than a diffuser. This is a model
choice rather than a measurement: real photo paper is not a true mirror and has
a diffuse component, and its $R(\lambda)$ is again fitted (no measured data),
so this is the reflector whose optical model is least constrained by independent
data.

\subsection{Config 4: wavelength-shifting fibre clamp}
\label{sec:wls}

The fourth layout follows the single-SiPM, WLS-readout variant of
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. Four \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter}-diameter Kuraray Y-11(200)
multi-clad wavelength-shifting fibres~\cite{KurarayPSF,Alekseev2022} run the full
\SI{250}{\milli\meter} length of the $-x$ narrow face in a single row. Over most
of the length they are parallel at a \SI{2.5}{\milli\meter} centre-to-centre
pitch---so a solid web of clamp material separates the milled
\SI{1.1}{\milli\meter} channels---and converge over a \SI{41}{\milli\meter}
length (\SI{39}{\milli\meter} ramp plus a \SI{2}{\milli\meter} stub) to a
touching \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} bundle that presents
all four ends to a single \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter} SiPM at
the $-y$ end; their far $+y$ ends are closed by individual aluminium mirror caps.
Each fibre is modelled as a multi-clad guide: a Y-11 core ($n = 1.59$) inside a
PMMA inner cladding ($n = 1.49$) inside a low-index fluoropolymer outer sheath
($n = 1.42$)---the low-index staircase that sustains core--cladding total
internal reflection. In the production geometry the fibre solid is an analytic,
mitered set of \texttt{G4CutTubs} legs (one per straight segment of the bent
centreline), placed so adjoining legs meet at exact miter planes; this
facet-free construction is what the runs use. (An earlier tessellated swept
cylinder, which occasionally produced stuck optical tracks, is retained as an
optional visualisation-only variant.) A thin \SI{0.10}{\milli\meter} grease film
couples the plate face to the clamp, and each fibre protrudes
\SI{0.05}{\milli\meter} through its channel mouth into that film, keeping the
grease continuous against the scintillator. The deliberate
\SI{0.05}{\milli\meter} air annulus between the \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} fibre and
its \SI{1.1}{\milli\meter} channel is essential: the fibre must not touch the
clamp ($n = 1.55$), and the outer sheath ($n = 1.42$) needs the low-index
clearance to keep guiding the light. The clamp body is 3D-printed white plastic,
modelled by default as a Lambertian reflector with \SI{85}{\percent} reflectivity
applied to \emph{every} clamp face (channel walls and grease-facing front
included), so light leaking out of the fibre is returned rather than lost. The
far-end mirror is a full aluminium disc whose logical volume carries a reflecting
skin as well as the fibre--cap border surfaces, so that light travelling in the
\emph{core}---not only the cladding---is also turned around at the far end. WLS
re-emission is drawn from the digitized Y-11 emission spectrum with an
exponential delay of \SI{7}{\nano\second} (Sec.~\ref{sec:wlphysics}), and the
\texttt{G4OpWLS} process is enabled automatically when this geometry is built. In
the report's top-down frame the clamp runs along the \emph{top} edge with the
single read-out SiPM at the \emph{top-left} corner, as in Fig.~3(b) of
Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. Unlike the side-mounted sensors of configs~1--3, this
read-out SiPM is coupled to the slab as well as to the fibres. Its
\SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter} grease pad is centred at
$(x,y)=(-125.55,-125.05)\,\si{\milli\meter}$ and therefore overlaps the slab's
$-y$ face by \SI{1.45}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter}
(\SI{5.8}{\milli\meter\squared}); this is a real optical contact, not a gap.
The contact is implemented with a pair of \texttt{G4LogicalBorderSurface}
objects carrying the polished Fresnel coupling surface in both directions, so
that it locally overrides the reflector skin and the SiPM sees direct blue
scintillation light through the pad. The direct path is enabled by default
(\texttt{setWLSPadSlabContact true}); setting it to \texttt{false} restores the
legacy fibre-only model, in which the pad is separated from the slab and the
SiPM receives only wavelength-shifted light from the fibre ends.

\subsection{Optical physics implementation}
\label{sec:physics}

Optical photons are transported by the standard \gfour\ optical physics
module~\cite{Geant4PRM}, with scintillation, \v{C}erenkov, bulk
absorption, Rayleigh scattering and boundary processes all active. Multiple
scattering (Mie) is disabled; wavelength shifting (the \texttt{G4OpWLS} process)
is enabled only for the config-4 fibre layout (Sec.~\ref{sec:wls}) and inert for
all others. The boundary physics of the \gfour\ unified surface model, the
\texttt{RINDEX} requirements it imposes, and the three code paths used by the
four finishes are described in Appendix~\ref{app:g4}, where they are verified
line-by-line against the \gfour\ source. This subsection states the two
process-specific pieces of the model: the wavelength shifter and the
photocathode.

\subsection{Wavelength shifting}
\label{sec:wlphysics}

In the config-4 layout, photons that reach the fibre cores are absorbed and
re-emitted isotropically at longer wavelength by the \texttt{G4OpWLS} process,
which is configured through the fibre material's \texttt{WLSABSLENGTH},
\texttt{WLSCOMPONENT} and \texttt{WLSTIMECONSTANT} properties. Both spectra are
the digitized Kuraray Y-11(200) curves: the absorption band peaks at
\SI{430}{\nano\meter} (onset $\approx\SI{486}{\nano\meter}$) and the re-emission
peak is \SI{476}{\nano\meter} (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra_opt}). The absolute
absorption strength is set by the \SI{430}{\nano\meter} absorption length,
\SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} in production (with a systematic band of
\SIrange{0.3}{3}{\milli\meter} available through the
\texttt{setWLSAbsLength} command, but not scanned here; see
Sec.~\ref{sec:caveats}). The re-emission is sampled with an
exponential time profile, $\tau = \SI{7}{\nano\second}$, rather than a fixed
delay. \texttt{G4OpWLS} samples the secondary from \texttt{WLSCOMPONENT} subject
to $E_\gamma' \le E_\gamma$, so the emission table is cut at the absorption
onset; a small overlap of the emission tail with the absorption band gives mild
self-absorption. The process is switched on by the physics list when the WLS
geometry is detected (the \gfour\ optical-physics constructor reads the process
activation at build time, so the flag is set before the base construction).
Because most of the shifted emission lies red-ward of the absorption band, the
re-emitted light is transported along the fibre by TIR to the read-out
SiPM---which is why the detected config-4 spectrum is green-shifted (mean
$\sim\!\SI{510}{\nano\meter}$) relative to the direct-coupled configurations.

\subsection{Photodetection: the SiPM photocathode}

Photodetection is delegated entirely to \gfour, rather than being drawn
manually in the sensitive detector. The grease/SiPM boundary is a
\texttt{dielectric\_metal} surface whose MPT carries the SiPM
\texttt{EFFICIENCY} as a function of photon energy (equivalently, of the
wavelength $\lambda$), i.e.\ the photon detection
efficiency (PDE) of an ASD-NUV4S-P-40 device~\cite{AdvanSiD_NUV}, peaking at
$0.431$ near \SI{410}{\nano\meter} (the digitized 25-point curve of
Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra_opt}; values fall to $\approx 0.30$ at
\SI{490}{\nano\meter}), with \texttt{REFLECTIVITY} $=0$.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely because it is the reason the sensitive
detector needs no random draw of its own. In \texttt{DielectricMetal()},
when the Fresnel--PDE roll places a photon in the ``absorbed''
channel, \texttt{DoAbsorption()} is called. Inside it:
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{status}} =
  \begin{cases}
    \mathrm{Absorption}, & \xi \ge \operatorname{EFFICIENCY}(\lambda),\\[2pt]
    \mathrm{Detection},  & \xi <  \operatorname{EFFICIENCY}(\lambda),
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \xi \sim U(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{EFFICIENCY}(\lambda)$ is the tabulated PDE. The absorbed
photon is assigned a non-zero
\texttt{LocalEnergyDeposit} \emph{only} when the PDE draw succeeds. Back in
\texttt{PostStepDoIt}, if the status is \texttt{Detection} \emph{and} the
invoke-SD flag is set, the sensitive detector is invoked:
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{status}} = \mathrm{Detection}\ \wedge\ f_{\mathrm{InvokeSD}}
  \ \Longrightarrow\ \operatorname{InvokeSD}(\text{step}).
\end{equation}
The flag is enabled in the physics list via
\texttt{G4OpticalParameters::SetBoundaryInvokeSD(true)}. Consequently the
sensitive-detector \texttt{ProcessHits} fires \emph{only} for genuine
photoelectrons (it tests that the step energy deposit is positive), counts one
photoelectron per call, and kills the photon (the SiPM is opaque). This is a clean
separation of concerns: the physics of the photocathode lives in the optical
surface, and the sensitive detector only books hits.

\subsection{Per-event optical accounting}

Two per-event counts recur throughout this report. Write $\pe^{(e,s)}$ for the
number of photoelectrons detected in SiPM $s$ during event $e$, and
$\ngam^{(e)}$ for the number of optical photons generated in the slab during
event $e$ (scintillation plus \v{C}erenkov), irrespective of their later fate.

A thread-local diagnostic record (\texttt{OpticalDiag}) accumulates, per event:
scintillation photons, \v{C}erenkov photons, terminated photons, detected
photoelectrons, and photons that escaped to the world. The primary muon's
first-entry position in the slab is captured on the first step inside the
scintillator logical volume and stored in slab-local coordinates. At end of
event the sensitive detector writes one row per (event, SiPM), \emph{including
zero-detection rows}, so the sample is unbiased in the muon interaction
position. Each row carries the entry position, the event's produced-photon
count (the efficiency denominator), the detected count in that SiPM, the first
arrival time, the mean and RMS detected wavelength, and the track weight. Three
variable-length branches additionally list the individual photoelectrons: their
global arrival times (\texttt{tDetected}), wavelengths
(\texttt{wlDetected\_nm}) and creating processes (\texttt{creatorDetected}, with
$1 =$ \texttt{OpWLS}, $2 =$ scintillation, $3 =$ \v{C}erenkov). The per-row
count, timing and spectral data therefore support per-SiPM, combined and
spatially-binned collection-efficiency analyses together with offline
pulse-shape and process-mix studies.

Figure~\ref{fig:vis} shows a head-less event display of a single vertical muon
in the glossy-wrapped plate (config~1). For legibility the display uses a
\SI{30}{\mega\electronvolt} muon---just below the \v{C}erenkov threshold of the
scintillator---with the scintillation yield rescaled, so that the individual
optical tracks can be followed: photons are generated along the muon path,
propagate by total internal reflection and specular reflection off the wrapper,
and terminate on the SiPM patches at the four edges.

\subsection{Beam, irradiation patches, and efficiency definition}

The primary source is a vertical muon pencil beam: a \SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{50}{\milli\meter}
square beam of \SI{4}{\giga\electronvolt} $\mu^-$ normally incident on the plate,
with the beam centre placed at three positions, each irradiating a
\SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{50}{\milli\meter} patch lying \emph{entirely
on} the polished plate. With the plate spanning $[-125,125]\,\si{\milli\meter}$
in both $x$ and $y$, the patch centres are
\[
  \text{centre} = (0,0),\qquad
  \text{edge} = (0,-100),\qquad
  \text{corner} = (100,-100)\ \si{\milli\meter},
\]
so the illuminated regions are $[-25,25]^2$, $[-25,25]\times[-125,-75]$ and
$[75,125]\times[-125,-75]$ respectively (report frame: $+y$ is the top,
$-x$ the left). This places the edge and corner regions on the bottom
($-y$) edge, opposite the single instrumented face of config~3 (top, $+y$),
and it matches the \SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{50}{\milli\meter}
active area of the muon coincidence detector (MCD) that defines the regions of
interest in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. All three patches have the same area, so the
denominator of Eq.~\ref{eq:eff} is the same at all three positions and the
three points of a curve are directly comparable. Each
(config, reflector, position) runs \num{2500} events after a single
\texttt{/run/initialize}. The matrix therefore comprises
\[
  5\ \text{configs} \times 4\ \text{reflectors} \times 3\ \text{positions}
  = 60\ \text{files}, \qquad 60 \times \num{2500} = \num{150000}\ \text{generated events}.
\]
Two focused calibration campaigns bracket the remaining surface inputs and are
presented first. A bare-contact scan
runs configs~1--3 at eleven contact fractions
$f=0$, $0.005$, $\dots$, $0.050$ at the three beam positions,
$3 \times 11 \times 3 = \num{99}$ files with \num{1000} events each
(\num{99000} events); an aluminium-backing scan runs configs~1--3 with
\texttt{grease} and \texttt{air} backing at the three positions,
$3 \times 2 \times 3 = \num{18}$ files with \num{1000} events each
(\num{18000} events). The Al scan is taken from the earlier production
campaign because the Al model is unchanged and that scan is complete; the
contact scan is the new extended campaign. Together with the whole-plate run of
Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} ($20 \times \num{25000} = \num{500000}$ events), the
campaign totals \num{767000} events, of which \num{267000} are matrix-style
($\text{matrix} + \text{contact} + \text{Al}$).

For Config 2 (mod) the corner patch is not entirely on the solid: the
\SI{12}{\milli\meter} chamfer removes a right triangle of
\SI{72}{\milli\meter\squared} from the $50\times\SI{50}{\milli\meter}$ patch,
i.e.\ \SI{2.88}{\percent} of it (and \SI{0.461}{\percent} of the whole plate,
split equally over the four corners). A muon that lands in a removed triangle
does not enter the scintillator and writes no \texttt{sipmHits} row. The
efficiency of Eq.~\ref{eq:eff} is a ratio of detected to \emph{produced}
photons for events that do enter, so it is unaffected by this loss; yields
quoted per generated event are \SI{2.88}{\percent} low at the corner and
\SI{0.46}{\percent} low in the whole-plate run. Whole-plate detection
probabilities use distinct event numbers, not row counts.

Production runs were executed on the HPC cluster.
The optional per-slab optical track-length flux map is disabled for the matrix
(it is retained as a separate diagnostic); the collection efficiency is
reconstructed entirely offline from the \texttt{sipmHits} tree at any desired
binning, so the map is not needed.

The collection efficiency is defined, for a set $\mathcal{S}$ of SiPMs and an
event $e$, as
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{e}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \pe^{(e,s)}}
                     {\displaystyle\sum_{e}\ngam^{(e)}},
  \label{eq:eff}
\end{equation}
where the outer sum runs over all simulated events and the inner sum over the
SiPM set $\mathcal{S}$. The all-SiPM sum over the three beam
positions is the per-position figure of merit. A \emph{plate-averaged}
efficiency is the appropriate global figure of merit, but the three patches
sample only part of the plate; it is therefore obtained from the uniform
whole-plate irradiation of Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} and reported there.

For presentation, all position-resolved results (the maps of
Fig.~\ref{fig:irr} and the whole-slab maps
of Sec.~\ref{sec:uniformity}) are shown in the report frame with the top edge at $+y$,
the bottom at $-y$, the left at $-x$ and the right at $+x$. The internal
simulation frame is this view rotated by \SI{90}{\degree} clockwise; the primary
muon entry points and the SiPM markers are mapped into the report frame by
$(x,y)\to(y,-x)$ before plotting. This rotation is a rigid change of view and
leaves every scalar result (yields, spectra, efficiencies) unchanged; it only
fixes the orientation of config~3 (top edge) and of the edge/corner irradiation
sites (bottom edge).

The optical measurements do not fix two remaining surface inputs: the effective
bare-holder contact fraction $f$ and the aluminium backing. We determine both
before presenting any production result, so that every matrix and whole-slab
yield below uses one consistent nominal surface model. Both calibrations use the
same beam patches and efficiency definition just described.

\subsection{Bare-plate holder contact}
\label{sec:contact}

The clean bare model assumes a \SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} air gap between the
polished plate and the black holder. A real clamped plate touches the holder at
scattered high spots, and each contact frustrates total internal reflection and
removes trapped light. To bracket this unmeasured loss channel we repeat the
three-patch scan for a mean-field contact fraction $f$: every hit on a bare face
is absorbed with probability $f$ and otherwise follows the normal Fresnel/TIR
path, implemented as a polished \texttt{dielectric\_dielectric} skin with
\texttt{REFLECTIVITY} $=1-f$ and \texttt{TRANSMITTANCE} $=0$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:refl-bare}). The extended scan covers configs~1--3, the three beam
positions and eleven fractions $f=0$, $0.005$, $\dots$, $0.050$, with \num{1000}
events per case ($99$ files, \num{99000} events). It is an effective area
fraction, not a measurement of the holder gap.

Figure~\ref{fig:contact} shows the absolute yield versus $f$, with the matrix
TiO$_2$ reference drawn as dashed lines, and the normalised centre yield.
At $f=0$ the config-1 bare plate collects \num{304.95}, \num{295.37} and
\num{288.55}~\pe/event at the centre, edge and corner; the corresponding config-2
values are \num{160.21}, \num{153.68} and \num{152.25}, and the config-3 values
\num{81.27}, \num{79.20} and \num{80.23}. A 1\% contact fraction already removes
about half the light: at the config-1 centre the yield falls to \num{155.01}
\pe/event, and at the nominal $f=\num{0.045}$ to \num{74.37}. The loss is therefore
strongly non-linear in the first few percent of contact area, as expected when a
small fraction of the surface provides a fast escape path for the trapped mode
volume.

The nominal value is chosen from the crossings themselves, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:contactcross}. At the centre the crossing is remarkably stable
across layouts, $f=\num{0.0167}$--$\num{0.0172}$ for configs~1--3. At the edge
and corner the required fraction drops to \num{0.0034}--\num{0.0088} in the
four-sided layouts, where the diffuse TiO$_2$ reference is bright, while in
the single-sided config~3 it rises to \num{0.0387} (edge) and \num{0.0408}
(corner), because the TiO$_2$ edge and corner patches there are the dimmest of
the three positions. No single $f$ matches every cell exactly; the production
nominal $f=\num{0.045}$ is the smallest sampled fraction that places all nine
cells below TiO$_2$, at the cost of over-suppressing the four-sided centre. At
that value the nine bare yields are
\num{74.37}/\num{64.42}/\num{63.25} (cfg~1),
\num{37.60}/\num{32.35}/\num{32.04} (cfg~2) and
\num{19.22}/\num{16.32}/\num{16.04} (cfg~3) for centre/edge/corner.
The same global $f$ thus matches the ordering in every layout and position; we
retain $f=0$ as the clean-air reference and lower bound on the loss.

\subsection{Aluminium backing: grease versus air}
\label{sec:albacking}

The aluminium foil is the one wrapper whose optical contact is explicitly
ambiguous in the reference construction: grease is applied in places, but
wrinkles and trapped air leave other regions decoupled. The production model
uses the \texttt{air} backing ($n=1.0003$, critical angle
\SI{39.3}{\degree} at the scintillator), which leaves the full TIR cone intact,
so only the sub-critical fraction reaches the foil. The alternative
\texttt{grease} backing ($n=1.46$, critical angle \SI{67.5}{\degree}) lets most
trapped light refract into the grease and return specularly from the foil; it is
retained as the lower-bound systematic. Both use the same tabulated aluminium
reflectance ($R=0.904$--$0.925$). The backing scan covers configs~1--3 at the
three positions, with \num{1000} events per case, using
\texttt{/dcs-monitor/det/muonScint/setAlBacking air|grease}. It is taken from
the earlier complete production campaign because the Al model is unchanged; the
v6p5 matrix air-backed centre value (\num{482.23}~pe/event) agrees with the v5
scan value (\num{480.05}) to better than \SI{0.5}{\percent}.

Figure~\ref{fig:albacking} and Table~\ref{tab:albacking} show the result. Air
backing raises the aluminium yield by a factor \num{2.38}--\num{2.59} depending
on layout and position. At the config-1 centre the foil goes from \num{201.92}
to \num{480.05}~pe/event, at the config-2 centre from \num{102.51} to
\num{254.57}, and at the config-3 centre from \num{51.98} to \num{127.90}. The
air-backed foil reaches \SI{92.2}{\percent}, \SI{92.5}{\percent} and
\SI{90.9}{\percent} of the glossy yield from the production matrix
(Sec.~\ref{sec:yield}) in those three cases, i.e.\ it recovers
the measured near-equality of aluminium and glossy paper. The grease-backed
branch is roughly a factor \num{2.4} lower---about \SI{37}{\percent}--\SI{39}{\percent} of the
glossy yield---and would put the foil below the clean-air ($f=0$) bare plate, so
the backing choice is the dominant Al systematic. The
enhancement is slightly larger at the edge and corner than at the centre for
config~3, but the position dependence within each backing remains weak.

\subsection{The three-patch matrix scan}

Figure~\ref{fig:yield} shows the per-event optical-photon yield (the
denominator of Eq.~\ref{eq:eff}) for the four reflectors. The mean yield is
essentially independent of the reflector---about \num{19600} photons per event---which is the correct physical behaviour: the reflector changes how
photons are transported, not how many are produced. The small spread reflects
event-to-event variation in the muon path length.

Figure~\ref{fig:detected} shows the corresponding photoelectron spectra. Here
the reflector has a clear effect: at the config-1 centre the calibrated model

aluminium and \num{520.7}\,\pe/event for glossy paper. The ordering is
$\text{bare}<\text{TiO}_2<\text{Al}<\text{glossy}$, with the air-backed foil
close to glossy paper. The bare curve is therefore a broad, low-occupancy
distribution rather than the full-cone TIR distribution obtained at $f=0$,
which still loses the whole sub-critical fraction at the black wall; the
difference is the contact-loss systematic of Sec.~\ref{sec:contact}.

The efficiency scale is set by the SiPM PDE and the coupling: of order
\num{19600} photons are produced and the best wrapper delivers
$\approx 521$ photoelectrons, i.e.\ $\approx 2.65\%$. Given a PDE of
$\sim\!0.4$ and the geometric acceptance of a side-mounted sensor, this is
consistent.

The three irradiation patches separate the response by position, and the
tempting single-coordinate summary is the distance $r$ of the patch
from the plate centre, but $r$ is blind to azimuth, and the shortcoming is
decisive here: configs~3 and~4 instrument a \emph{single} face, so the collection
depends on the \emph{direction} from which the plate is irradiated, not merely on
the distance. All three patches also lie on the half of the plate opposite the
instrumented face of the single-sided layouts, so their distances are not even
monotonic in the proximity to a sensor. We therefore keep the three patches
separate and compare them directly.

Figure~\ref{fig:poslayouts} groups the three patches by layout (one panel per

is that the specular wrappers are nearly \emph{flat} across the three patches
in the four Table~IV layouts: there the maximum/minimum patch ratio spans
\SIrange{1.0}{4.2}{\percent} for aluminium and \SIrange{0.8}{3.6}{\percent} for
glossy paper. Config~2 (mod) is the exception, because its corner patch sits
next to an instrumented chamfer; including it, the ratios reach
\SI{20.5}{\percent} (Al) and \SI{17.3}{\percent} (glossy). The diffuse
TiO$_2$ paint is the opposite extreme, varying by
\SIrange{74.9}{257.9}{\percent} (a factor \num{1.75}--\num{3.58}). The
contact-limited bare plate lies between them:
\SIrange{8.3}{164.8}{\percent}, with the four-sided layouts~1 and~2 and
config~3 near \SI{17}{\percent}, config~4 at \SI{8.3}{\percent}, and the
corner-cut layout at \SI{164.8}{\percent}.

The \emph{sign} of the position trend is wrapper- and layout-dependent, and
this is the first place where the simulation and the measurement part company.
For the bare plate, aluminium and glossy paper the three-patch ordering is
centre $>$ edge $>$ corner in the four Table~IV layouts, i.e.\ the corner is
the dimmest patch, exactly as reported in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. For the diffuse
TiO$_2$ paint the trend is \emph{reversed} in the four-sided layouts~1 and~2

Config~2 (mod) is a deliberate exception to the paper's corner rule: its
corner beam lands beside an instrumented chamfer, and the corner is therefore
the brightest patch for \emph{every} wrapper (corner/centre ratios
\num{2.65} bare, \num{3.58} TiO$_2$, \num{1.21} Al and \num{1.17} glossy).
The mechanism behind the TiO$_2$ reversal is discussed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism}; it is
the same mechanism that sets the uniformity of the full-plate maps and the
ability of the SiPM pattern to localise the muon.

\subsection{The whole-plate average}

The matrix scan pools only three \SI{50}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{50}{\milli\meter}
patches, all on the $-y$ half of the plate, so the efficiency it yields depends
on the choice of patches and is \emph{not} a plate average. The whole-slab run
removes that bias: the GPS plane is expanded to
$250 \times \SI{250}{\milli\meter}$ (\texttt{halfx} $=$ \texttt{halfy} $=$
\SI{125}{\milli\meter}) and \num{25000} events are generated per
(config, reflector), so the primary muon's first-entry position is sampled
\emph{uniformly} over the entire plate and the pooled ratio
$\sum\pe/\sum\ngam$ is the \emph{plate-averaged} collection efficiency---the
appropriate global figure of merit. Figure~\ref{fig:bars} shows it for every
(config, reflector) pair, and Table~\ref{tab:eff} lists the numbers.

With the calibrated surfaces every layout reproduces the measured ordering,
\[
  \text{bare} < \text{TiO}_2 < \text{Al} < \text{glossy}
  \quad(\text{all five layouts}),
\]
with glossy paper the brightest wrapper in every layout and air-backed
aluminium within \SI{92}{\percent} of its yield in the four Table~IV layouts
(\SI{95}{\percent} in config~4, \SI{91}{\percent} in Config 2 (mod)). The
contact-limited bare plate is now the weakest wrapper in every layout, as the
experiment finds. The spread from weakest to brightest is a factor
\num{6.5}--\num{6.9} in the four directly-coupled layouts, set mainly by the
bare contact loss, and \num{4.5} in config~4.
The efficiency scales roughly with the photosensitive area: the eight-SiPM
layout (config~1, $\eff = 4.081\times10^{-3}$) is highest, four SiPMs on
four faces (config~2, $2.068\times10^{-3}$) roughly halve it, and concentrating
the two sensors of config~3 on a single face ($1.048\times10^{-3}$) halves it
again. Config~2 (mod) has the same four sensors and active area as config~2,

cut removes \SI{0.46}{\percent} of the area; it reaches

sensors at the plate centre therefore does not recover the light sharing that
the side-mounted layout lacks. The single-SiPM WLS-fibre layout (config~4,

it is lower for TiO$_2$, Al and glossy by \SI{20}{\percent}--\SI{40}{\percent},
and now slightly lower for the bare plate as well ($1.007$ versus
$1.048\times10^{-3}$).

\subsection{Survival versus mixing: the two axes that organise every result}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The ordering of Table~\ref{tab:eff} is not a single effect, and it is worth
separating it into two, because they pull in opposite directions and because
almost every other result in this report follows from the same pair.

\emph{Survival} is the fraction of the trapped light that survives the
transport. It is governed by the critical angle at the plate's non-instrumented
faces and by what happens to the sub-critical fraction that leaves the guided
mode:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the bare plate keeps the full TIR cone, but the sub-critical
        \SI{39.3}{\degree} cone that crosses the air gap reaches the black
        holder and is absorbed---so the bare plate keeps the cone but loses
        everything outside it;
  \item the diffuse paint destroys the cone altogether: there is no TIR at a
        \texttt{groundfrontpainted} surface, and the light is re-emitted
        isotropically, so most of it is re-absorbed on the next encounter;
  \item the two mirrors keep the cone and return the sub-critical fraction
        with a reflectivity of \num{0.90}--\num{0.96}, which is why they are
        the brightest.
\end{itemize}
The bare plate is thus \emph{not} the brightest, even though it keeps the full
TIR cone, because the region outside the cone is exactly where a black holder
catches everything. Adding the contact loss of Sec.~\ref{sec:contact}---a loss
on \emph{every} bounce, not only on the sub-critical cone---takes it from
third-brightest to weakest, which is what the measurement requires.

\emph{Mixing} is the degree to which the light that survives has forgotten
where it was produced. A cavity bounded by a specular mirror and a TIR guide
is a good mixer; a cavity whose walls are Lambertian is a poor one, because
each re-emission randomises the direction and the light leaves towards
whichever wall is nearest, so the probability of reaching a sensor depends on
the geometry of the plate and on the sensor positions. The three-patch data of
Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} already shows the two extremes: in the four Table~IV
layouts the specular wrappers are flat to within
\SIrange{0.8}{4.2}{\percent} across the three patches, while the diffuse paint
varies by a factor \num{1.75}--\num{2.21}. Config 2 (mod) exaggerates the
effect without changing its order: even there the paint varies by a factor
\num{3.58} against \num{1.21} (Al) and \num{1.17} (glossy), because the
corner sensor is much closer to the illuminated corner than to the centre.

The useful statement, and the one that organises the rest of the report, is
that these two axes are \emph{not} independent. The wrappers that survive
well (glossy, Al) mix well, because both properties come from the same
specular+TIR boundary; the wrapper that mixes badly (the paint) survives
badly, for the same reason. The bare plate is the interesting case: it mixes
\emph{excellently} (it is a perfect TIR guide, like the two mirrors) but
survives badly (absorbing wall, plus the contact loss), and it is the only
wrapper for which the two axes disagree. This is visible in the data as a bare plate that is
dim, yet whose response still varies with position by a few to
\SI{19}{\percent}---the residual structure of a cavity that mixes well but
not perfectly.

The consequence for the position reconstruction of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon} is
direct: light sharing carries position information exactly when the mixing is
poor, so the diffuse paint---the dimmest of the reflective wrappers and the
least uniform---is the only one whose \emph{integrated charges} localise the
muon, while the bright, perfectly mixing specular wrappers have to be read out
in time instead.

\subsection{Full-plate maps}

The same whole-slab events that give the plate average of
Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} also resolve the response by entry position.
Reconstructing the efficiency from the
\texttt{sipmHits} tree in $5 \times \SI{5}{\milli\meter}$ cells gives the field
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon(x,y) = \frac{\sum_{e\,:\,(x_e,y_e)\in\text{cell}}\ \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \pe^{(e,s)}}
                          {\sum_{e\,:\,(x_e,y_e)\in\text{cell}}\ \ngam^{(e)}},
\end{equation}
where the sums run over the whole-slab events whose primary-muon first-entry
position $(x_e,y_e)$ falls inside the cell, i.e.\ identically to
Eq.~\ref{eq:eff} but binned in the entry position.

Figure~\ref{fig:wholeslab} shows the complete set of full-plate maps for
config~1, one panel per reflector. The plate-averaged efficiencies are
$4.081\times10^{-3}$ (bare), $9.551\times10^{-3}$ (TiO$_2$),
$2.445\times10^{-2}$ (Al) and $2.649\times10^{-2}$ (glossy). The contact-limited

\subsection{A single number for the spread: \texorpdfstring{$\kappa_{\rm 2D}$}{kappa 2D}}

A purely radial contrast is deliberately \emph{not} used.  The read-out layouts
have no continuous rotational symmetry---configs~1, 2 and 2 (mod) are four-fold
symmetric, configs~3 and~4 single-sided---and the plate is square, so the
collection efficiency is not a function of radius alone.  Beyond the inscribed
circle ($r > \SI{125}{\milli\meter}$) only the four corner wedges of the plate
are populated, so a fixed-width radial bin would mix a shrinking range of
directions and its radial profile $\varepsilon(r)$ would reflect the geometry of
the square rather than the detector.  The uniformity is therefore quantified
directly in the plate plane $(x,y)$.

The \emph{two-dimensional contrast}
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_{\rm 2D} \;=\; \frac{\max_{\rm cell}\varepsilon}{\min_{\rm cell}\varepsilon}
\end{equation}
collapses the field to a single number, the ratio of the brightest to the
darkest region; it answers ``how large is the spread'' and is the quantity that
ranks the layouts and reflectors.  $\kappa_{\rm 2D}$ is evaluated on a coarser
$5\times5$ grid of \SI{50}{\milli\meter} cells, because on the
\SI{5}{\milli\meter} cells of the maps the per-cell statistics are too thin for
a stable max/min---Poisson noise in the extreme cells would inflate it.  The
measure is therefore deliberately the \emph{robust} dynamic range, and is
smaller than the raw peak-to-trough range of the fine maps.  No symmetry is
assumed, so
$\kappa_{\rm 2D}$ is meaningful for the single-sided layouts~3 and~4 as well as
for the four-sided ones.

The direction-sensitive contrast $\kappa_{\rm 2D}$ ranks the reflectors
consistently in \emph{every} layout,
\[
\text{TiO}_2 \;>\; \text{bare} \;>\; \text{Al} \;\gtrsim\; \text{glossy},
\]
i.e.\ the diffuse TiO$_2$ paint is the \emph{least} uniform wrapper and glossy
paper the most uniform. The diffuse paint is also the dimmest direct-coupled
wrapper, while the bright specular wrappers are the most uniform: the specular
transport mixes the light so thoroughly that the entry position is largely
erased ($\kappa_{\rm 2D}\approx1.1$--\num{1.4}), whereas the diffuse paint
retains a pronounced positional response. The contact-limited bare plate sits
between them because the holder loss is itself position dependent.
Numerically the TiO$_2$ contrast grows from $\kappa_{\rm 2D} = \num{1.911}$,
\num{2.579} and \num{3.688} in the four-sided layouts~1, 2 and 2 (mod) to
\num{8.413} and \num{10.897} in the single-sided layouts~3 and~4; the bare
plate spans \num{1.56}--\num{4.14}, aluminium \num{1.07}--\num{1.37} and
glossy \num{1.07}--\num{1.36}. A muon crossing anywhere on the plate is
therefore collected within about \SI{40}{\percent} of the most favourable
point for Al/glossy, within a factor of \num{4.1} for the contact-limited bare
plate, and within a factor of \num{10.9} for TiO$_2$ in the single-sided
layouts. Figure~\ref{fig:wholecontrast} collects all twenty values as bar
charts, one panel per layout; Table~\ref{tab:wholerefl} lists the same values
numerically, resolving the near-unity entries that the linear bar scale
cannot.

\subsection{The shape of the field: distance to the nearest sensor}
\label{sec:nearest}

The contrast number of the previous subsection says how large the spread is,
but not what shape it has, and the maps show that the shape is not radial at
all. For config~1 the bare plate maps to a \emph{checkerboard}: the
$50\times\SI{50}{\milli\meter}$ cell efficiencies alternate between
$3.3\times10^{-3}$ and $5.2\times10^{-3}$ from cell to cell, with the bright
cells being exactly those that contain a SiPM. For config~2 the bare plate
shows the same thing more sharply: the four cells that contain the four sensors
read $3.21$--$3.42\times10^{-3}$ while the remaining twenty-one read
$1.65$--$2.07\times10^{-3}$. For config~3 the bare plate has two bright cells
($2.20\times10^{-3}$ and $2.45\times10^{-3}$) at the two sensor positions, but
the cell \emph{between} them---on the instrumented face, but not on a sensor---
is the darkest of the whole plate ($0.59\times10^{-3}$): light produced there
leaves through the uninstrumented part of that face before it can reach a
sensor. The diffuse TiO$_2$ paint shows
the complementary structure: a bright frame, with the cells adjacent to the
sensors reaching $1.215\times10^{-2}$ and the central cross falling to
$6.4$--$8.0\times10^{-3}$.

These patterns are all produced by the same rule, and the rule has a single
coordinate: \emph{the response tracks, on average, the distance from the
interaction point to the nearest collection site}. Where the cavity mixes the
light well, the response is independent of that distance; where it mixes
poorly, the response falls off as the interaction point moves away from the
sensors, because the light then has a longer and more lossy path to reach one.
The rule is a statement about the \emph{envelope} of the map and not a
point-by-point description of it: the cell between the two sensors of config~3
quoted above is a counterexample to a strict monotonicity, and the reason is
visible---that cell lies on the instrumented face, so its light leaves the
plate immediately through the part of that face that carries no sensor. The
distance statistic below integrates over such configurations, so what it
measures is the trend that survives the averaging.

Figure~\ref{fig:nearest} tests this directly, by binning the whole-slab
events in the distance $d$ to the nearest collection site and plotting the
efficiency normalised to the plate average against it. For the four
direct-coupled layouts the collection sites are the SiPM tiles; for config~4
the collection aperture is the fibre clamp, a line along the top edge, so $d$
is the distance to that line. The four curves of each panel share one shape
but not one level:
\begin{itemize}
  \item in the four Table~IV layouts the two specular wrappers are flat to
        within \SI{5}{\percent} over the whole range---they are near-perfect
        light mixers. In Config 2 (mod) the first bin is
        $1.28\times$ (Al) and $1.25\times$ (glossy) the plate average,
        because the nearest sensor is only about \SI{27}{\milli\meter} from
        the corner;
  \item the contact-limited bare plate shows a clear excess in the first
        \SI{25}{\milli\meter} ($1.44\times$, $2.12\times$, $2.72\times$ and
        $3.36\times$ the plate average for configs~1, 2, 2 (mod) and~3) and
        then relaxes to slightly \emph{below} unity, which is the signature
        of a cavity that mixes well but loses a little light on every extra
        bounce;
  \item the diffuse paint shows the largest excess ($1.63\times$,
        $2.40\times$, $3.32\times$ and $4.52\times$) and the steepest decay,
        reaching $0.36$--$0.37$ of the average at the far side of the plate
        in the single-sided layouts and $0.59$--$0.63$ in Config 2 (mod).
\end{itemize}
This single figure therefore contains the explanation of the whole uniformity
result: the specular wrappers are uniform because they mix, the paint is
non-uniform because it does not, and the bare plate sits in between because
the contact loss destroys the perfect mixing of the air-gap guide without
destroying its geometry. It also explains the sign flip of the position trend
seen in the three-patch scan: in the four-sided layouts the corner patch is
close to two sensors and is therefore the brightest, and in Config 2 (mod) it
is close to one sensor and still brighter than the centre; in the single-sided
layouts the sensors are on the far side of the plate and the centre of the
plate---the point furthest from any sensor---is not the dimmest point, because
what matters is the distance to the \emph{nearest} sensor, and the single
sensor still beats the far corners.

\subsection{Wavelength of detected photons}

Figure~\ref{fig:wavelength} shows the wavelength distribution of detected
photoelectrons, weighted by their count, for the four reflectors. The
distribution peaks near \SI{456}{\nano\meter} for the reflective wrappers
(config-1 centre means of \SI{456.86}{\nano\meter} for glossy,
\SI{456.15}{\nano\meter} for Al and \SI{454.45}{\nano\meter} for TiO$_2$), i.e.\
slightly red-shifted with respect to the \SI{425}{\nano\meter} emission peak.
This is the expected consequence of self-absorption: the shorter-wavelength part
of the emission overlaps the absorption band and is attenuated over the
multi-bounce path, so the surviving and detected spectrum is red-shifted. The
contact-limited bare plate is bluer (\SI{449.46}{\nano\meter}) because the
holder-contact loss suppresses the longest multi-bounce paths before they can
redden the spectrum; its lower yield still leaves a well-defined distribution.

\subsection{Arrival times and pulse persistence}
\label{sec:timing}

The collection-efficiency maps above treat every detected photoelectron as a
point; the per-photoelectron timing columns of the \texttt{sipmHits} tree
let the \emph{arrival-time structure} of the detected light be examined as well.
Figure~\ref{fig:persistence} shows the oscilloscope-style persistence map of the
arrival-time profile for the five layouts (glossy paper). For each event the
per-SiPM arrival times are summed over the layout's sensors and histogrammed in
time, giving a trace of photoelectron count versus arrival time; stacking events
and recording, for every \SI{0.1}{\nano\second} time bin, how many events showed
a given count, yields a two-dimensional map whose abscissa is the arrival time, whose
ordinate is the instantaneous photoelectron count, and whose colour is the number
of events. The rising edge and the decaying tail therefore show how the detected
light builds up and dies away, and the vertical spread at a given time is the
event-to-event fluctuation of the instantaneous rate. For the four directly-read
layouts the modal mean arrival times are \SI{3.85}{\nano\second},
\SI{3.95}{\nano\second}, \SI{3.70}{\nano\second} and
\SI{3.75}{\nano\second} (configs~1, 2, 2 (mod), 3); the prompt peaks reach
\num{54}, \num{43}, \num{50} and \num{47} photoelectrons per
\SI{0.1}{\nano\second} bin, well above the \num{30} photoelectrons per bin that
the shared ordinate of the figure can show, so the top of the prompt peak is
clipped in the display; the single-fibre WLS read-out (config~4) peaks markedly
later, at \SI{7.95}{\nano\second} and at only \num{18} photoelectrons per bin,
which is therefore fully visible. The fractions arriving within
\SI{5}{\nano\second} are \num{31.6}, \num{30.4}, \num{31.0}, \num{29.8} and
\SI{13.4}{\percent} for configs~1, 2, 2 (mod), 3 and~4, within
\SI{10}{\nano\second} \num{63.2}, \num{61.3}, \num{62.2}, \num{60.2} and
\SI{40.3}{\percent}, while the late ($t>\SI{20}{\nano\second}$) tail carries
\num{10.7}, \num{12.3}, \num{11.6}, \num{13.1} and \SI{21.5}{\percent} of the
photoelectrons.

\section{Position reconstruction from the SiPM pattern}
\label{sec:recon}
% =====================================================================

The eight SiPMs of config~1 view the same scintillation event from eight
directions.  A natural question for a large-area muon trigger built this way is
whether the \emph{relative} pattern of the eight channels---their integrated
charges, their arrival-time wavefronts, and the prompt fraction of their
charge---carries enough information to localise the interaction point inside
the plate.  The whole-plate irradiation of Sec.~\ref{sec:yield} is the data set
that answers it: it samples the interaction point uniformly over the
\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{250}{\milli\meter} footprint and records, for
every event, both the ground truth (the muon's first-entry position) and the
complete per-SiPM photoelectron timestamp list.

\subsection{What the eight channels measure}
\label{sec:recon-method}

For every event the \texttt{sipmHits} tree carries, per SiPM $s \in \{0,\dots,7\}$,
the number of detected photoelectrons $N_s$, the ordered arrival times
$t_{(1),s} \le t_{(2),s} \le \dots \le t_{(N_s),s}$ of the individual
photoelectrons in the variable-length branch \texttt{tDetected}, the number of
photoelectrons that arrived within \SI{3}{\nano\second} of the interaction
$N^{\mathrm{prompt}}_s$ (the prompt fraction), and the mean detected
wavelength.  These are the optical counterparts of the quantities a read-out
chain provides: an integrated charge, a leading-edge or constant-fraction
timestamp, and a pulse-shape parameter.

It is worth being explicit about what $N_s$ is, because the distinction
matters for the electronics.  $N_s$ counts \emph{every} photoelectron detected
in the channel over the whole event, so it is the \emph{integral} of the light
pulse delivered to that SiPM (multiplied by the gain), not the amplitude of the
pulse peak.  This is the natural choice: the integral carries the full
Poisson statistics of the collected charge, whereas a peak-amplitude
measurement uses only the prompt subset of the light and is additionally
biased low by the spread of the photoelectron arrival times.  In the present
data the prompt fraction (photoelectrons arriving before \SI{3}{\nano\second})
is \SI{53.0}{\percent} of the light for the bare plate, \SI{33.7}{\percent} for
the TiO$_2$ paint and only \SIrange{15}{16}{\percent} for the two specular
wrappers, so a peak-amplitude read-out would work with a small minority of the
collected photons.  No peak-amplitude observable is used anywhere in this
section; the position information is extracted from pulse integrals and from
arrival times.

Only the transverse position can be reconstructed: the muon is vertical and
traverses the whole \SI{10}{\milli\meter} thickness, so the light is produced
along a vertical track and the side pattern carries no usable $z$ information
(the corresponding path-length difference between the two faces of the plate is
\SI{10}{\milli\meter}, i.e.\ \SI{0.05}{\nano\second}, an order of magnitude
below the timing signals below).

\subsection{A better clock than the first photon}
\label{sec:recon-cfd}

The simplest timing observable is the arrival time of the first photoelectron,
$t^{\mathrm{first}}_s = t_{(1),s}$.  It is convenient, but it is a poor clock,
for two reasons that act in opposite regimes.  A channel that collects more
photoelectrons triggers earlier simply because it has more chances to catch an
early photon: the shift of the trigger time scales as $N_s^{-1/2}$, the same
amplitude walk that a fixed-threshold discriminator suffers in an electronic
chain.  In the diffuse TiO$_2$ wrapper, where a corner muon delivers
$\sim 68$ photoelectrons to the two sensors at that corner and only $\sim 6$ to
the opposite sensors, this walk alone shifts $t^{\mathrm{first}}$ at the bright
sensors by several hundred picoseconds, with no relation whatsoever to the
distance of the muon.  In the opposite regime, a channel that collects only a
handful of photoelectrons is timed by a single photon, and its trigger time
fluctuates from event to event depending on whether one prompt photon happens
to enter the sensor aperture on its first pass.  The extreme-value statistics
that quantify the first effect are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:recon}.

The standard cure in an electronic read-out chain is a constant-fraction
discriminator, which triggers not on the amplitude of the pulse but on the
moment at which the pulse has reached a fixed fraction $\alpha$ of it
\cite{Gundacker2020}.  The exact optical counterpart is the \emph{wavefront
time} of the channel: the time by which a fraction $\alpha$ of the light of
that channel has arrived, i.e.\ the mean arrival time of the leading $\alpha$
fraction of its photoelectrons,
\begin{equation}
  \bar t_{\le\alpha,s}
  = \frac{1}{q_s}\!\left(
      \sum_{j=1}^{m_s} t_{(j),s}
      + (q_s - m_s)\,t_{(\min(N_s,\,m_s+1)),s}
    \right),
  \qquad
  q_s = \max(1,\,\alpha N_s), \quad m_s = \lfloor q_s \rfloor .
  \label{eq:tcfd-mean}
\end{equation}
Averaging over the leading photoelectrons removes the $N_s^{-1/2}$ amplitude
walk, because every channel is then timed on the same fraction of its own
pulse rather than on its first photon; and it suppresses the single-photon
shot noise, because the timing information is spread over the
$\lceil \alpha N_s\rceil \approx 8$ photoelectrons of the prompt component
instead of resting on one.  Below we call this the \emph{optical
constant-fraction (CFD) time} of the channel.

We use $\alpha = \SI{12.5}{\percent}$.  The choice is dictated by the cavity
physics of Sec.~\ref{sec:timing}: in the specular wrappers the prompt,
ballistic component carries \SIrange{15}{16}{\percent} of the total pulse
($\sim 8\text{--}10$ photoelectrons per channel) while the remaining
\SIrange{84}{85}{\percent} forms the spatially mixed multi-bounce
reverberation tail ($t > \SI{3}{\nano\second}$).  A high constant fraction
such as $\alpha = \SI{50}{\percent}$ therefore times the reverberating cavity
tail rather than the ballistic wavefront and progressively washes out the
timing information, whereas $\alpha = \SI{12.5}{\percent}$ integrates across
the ballistic rising edge just below the reverberation transition.

That argument is checked directly in Fig.~\ref{fig:recon-alpha}, which
measures the reconstruction error as a function of $\alpha$ over
$\alpha = \SIrange{3}{50}{\percent}$ for all four wrappers.  The result
confirms the picture and shows that the choice is not critical: over the whole
range $\SIrange{3}{20}{\percent}$ the error of the learned estimator changes by
less than \SI{6}{\percent} (e.g. \SI{16.9}{\milli\meter} at
$\alpha = \SI{3}{\percent}$ and \SI{16.2}{\milli\meter} at
$\alpha = \SI{12.5}{\percent}$ for the glossy wrapper), and the nominal value
is within \SI{1}{\percent} of the best one over the scanned range.  The
position of that minimum is not resolved more precisely than about
$\alpha = \SI{12.5}{\percent} \pm \SI{2.5}{\percent}$: the differences between
neighbouring points near the bottom of the curve ($\lesssim \SI{1}{\percent}$)
are of the order of the statistical precision of the error itself (about
\SI{1}{\percent} with \num{5000} test events).  The transparent linear
read-out is more sensitive,
because in the two specular wrappers it is the only channel that carries the
position information: there the error has a clear minimum at
$\alpha \simeq \SIrange{10}{15}{\percent}$ and grows by a factor
$\sim 1.5$ at $\alpha = \SI{50}{\percent}$ (from \SI{182}{\milli\meter} to
\SI{279}{\milli\meter} in glossy paper), which is exactly the loss of timing
sensitivity when the constant fraction reaches the reverberation tail.  The
bare plate and the diffuse paint, whose positions are set by the light
sharing and not by the timing, are flat in $\alpha$ throughout.  At
$\alpha = \SI{50}{\percent}$ the learned estimator still keeps most of its
resolution (from \SI{16.2}{\milli\meter} to \SI{16.4}{\milli\meter} in glossy
paper), because the charges and the prompt share continue to carry the
position information, whereas the transparent timing read-out loses half of
its sensitivity.

Panel~(a) of the figure measures what the argument is actually about: the
width of the CFD time difference at fixed muon position.  For the bare plate
and for the diffuse paint it is flat over the whole scan
($\SI{1.38}{\nano\second} \rightarrow \SI{1.34}{\nano\second}$ and
$\SI{0.89}{\nano\second} \rightarrow \SI{0.85}{\nano\second}$ between
$\alpha = \SI{3}{\percent}$ and \SI{50}{\percent}), as expected when the
arrival-time spread is set by the light sharing rather than by the ballistic
wavefront.  For the two specular wrappers the same width grows by about
\SI{70}{\percent} (glossy paper \SI{0.39}{\nano\second} $\rightarrow$
\SI{0.68}{\nano\second}, air-backed aluminium \SI{0.39}{\nano\second}
$\rightarrow$ \SI{0.67}{\nano\second}): beyond $\alpha \approx
\SI{20}{\percent}$ the wavefront time has become a tail time.  That the
linear resolution nevertheless \emph{improves} up to
$\alpha \approx \SI{15}{\percent}$ (panel~b) shows that it is not the width
alone that sets the resolution: over the same range the opposite-pair slope
$|\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{CFD},x}/\mathrm{d}x|$ grows from \SI{0.0018}{\nano\second\per\milli\meter}
to \SI{0.0029}{\nano\second\per\milli\meter} (glossy), i.e.\ faster than the
width, and only the ratio of the two --- the resolution itself --- has an
interior minimum.  Neither quantity alone is therefore a good tuning
criterion: the pulse width would drive $\alpha$ to its smallest scanned value,
the slope to $\alpha \approx \SI{20}{\percent}$.

Pairing the wavefront onset $t_{(1),s}$ with the leading-$\alpha$ wavefront
mean $\bar t_{\le\alpha,s}$ captures \emph{two} physical characteristics of the
pulse in each channel: the arrival time of the ballistic wavefront and its
\emph{optical rise time}
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t_{\mathrm{rise},s} = \bar t_{\le\alpha,s} - t_{(1),s},
  \label{eq:risetime}
\end{equation}
which measures the temporal dispersion of the rising edge (steep for a sensor
receiving a direct burst from a nearby muon, and broad for a distant sensor
accumulating multi-bounce light).

\subsection{Two ways to localise the muon}
\label{sec:recon-learned-method}

Two families of estimator are evaluated on the whole-plate dataset.  The first
is transparent and purely analytic: the \emph{opposite-pair} observables
\begin{equation}
  A_x = \frac{N_{\mathrm{right}}-N_{\mathrm{left}}}
             {N_{\mathrm{right}}+N_{\mathrm{left}}},
  \qquad
  T_x = \bar t_{\mathrm{right}}-\bar t_{\mathrm{left}},
  \qquad
  T_{\mathrm{CFD},x} = \bar t_{\le 12.5\%,\mathrm{right}}-\bar t_{\le 12.5\%,\mathrm{left}},
  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
formed from the two SiPMs on the right and the two on the left edge (and the
analogous $A_y$, $T_y$, $T_{\mathrm{CFD},y}$ for the bottom and top edges).
$N_{\mathrm{right}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{left}}$ are the summed pulse integrals of
the two channels on each edge, while $\bar t_{\mathrm{right}}$ and
$\bar t_{\le 12.5\%,\mathrm{right}}$ are the edge-averaged first-arrival and
$\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ wavefront times.  All three observables are odd
functions of the coordinate $x$, so a linear fit against $x$ measures the
first-order response slope $\partial O_x / \partial x$ and rms residual
$\sigma_O$, yielding an implied linear resolution
$\sigma_x = \sigma_O / |\partial O_x / \partial x|$.

Because the eight-channel pattern in a rectangular light guide is strongly
non-linear---particularly in the timing channel and near the plate corners---a
linear combination of opposite edges extracts only a fraction of the available
information.  The second estimator therefore learns the inverse map
$\mathbf{f} \mapsto \mathbf{y} = (x,y)\in[-L,L]^2$ ($L=\SI{125}{\milli\meter}$)
non-parametrically from a calibration training set of size
$N_{\mathrm{tr}}=\num{20000}$ (\SI{80}{\percent} of the \num{25000} whole-plate
events), and is evaluated on the held-out test set of size
$N_{\mathrm{te}}=\num{5000}$.  In words: for a new event, the estimator looks
for the $k=40$ calibration events whose eight-channel pattern looks most
similar, and returns their (known) positions, averaged with a weight that
falls off with the distance in pattern space,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t
  = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{k} w_m\,\mathbf{y}_{i_m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{k} w_m},
  \qquad
  w_m = K\!\left(\frac{d_m}{h}\right),
  \qquad
  K(u) = (1 - u^3)^3\,\Theta(1 - u),
  \label{eq:knn-simple}
\end{equation}
where $d_m$ is the distance of the $m$-th neighbour in pattern space, $h$ is
the distance of the farthest of the $k$ neighbours, and $\mathbf{y}_{i_m}$ is
the position of that neighbour, rotated or reflected into the frame of the
event by the symmetry that matched the two patterns.

Four ingredients make this work, and each of them has a plain reading:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The pattern is used in relative terms.}  The eight charges
        enter as shares of the total charge of the event, the eight arrival
        times as differences to the mean arrival time of the eight channels.
        The total light yield and the time of the interaction therefore cancel
        identically, and only the \emph{shape} of the pattern carries the
        position.
  \item \textbf{Each quantity is rescaled to a common spread} before the
        distances are computed, so that a quantity with a large spread cannot
        dominate the comparison merely because of its units.
  \item \textbf{The eight symmetries of the square plate are used.}  A pattern
        measured in a corner of the plate has eight equivalent versions (four
        rotations, four reflections), and so does every calibration event.
        Comparing a new pattern against all eight versions of every
        calibration pattern multiplies the effective density of the
        calibration set by eight, which is what makes the corners and the
        edges of the plate as well determined as its centre.
  \item \textbf{Uninformative information is down-weighted.}  In a specular
        wrapper the pulse integrals carry no position information, and
        including them with the same weight as the arrival times would bury
        the informative part of the pattern in noise.  Each block of the
        pattern therefore enters with a weight set by its own reconstruction
        error, so that a block that does not help is suppressed automatically.
\end{enumerate}
The formal definitions of the pattern blocks, of the symmetry-augmented
distance and of the block weights are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:recon}.

For the central muon the eight waveforms are indistinguishable: every SiPM

\subsection{Linear opposite-pair response: first hit versus optical CFD}
\label{sec:recon-linear}

Table~\ref{tab:reconobs} quantifies the linear read-out of Eq.~\ref{eq:pair},
comparing the pulse-integral asymmetry $A_x$, the first-arrival difference
$T_x$, and the $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical CFD wavefront difference
$T_{\mathrm{CFD},x}$.
For the TiO$_2$ paint the charge asymmetry is a clean, first-order position
observable: $A_x$ changes by \num{7.40e-3} per millimetre, so that the
\num{0.133} rms noise of $A_x$ corresponds to a position uncertainty of
\SI{18.0}{\milli\meter} along $x$ from a \emph{single} ratio of two pairs of
sensors.  For the two specular wrappers the charge slope is \num{5e-5}, i.e.\
two orders of magnitude smaller, because TIR mixing erases the first-order
charge gradient.

Comparing the two timing columns of Table~\ref{tab:reconobs} shows the effect
of the $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical CFD directly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For the specular wrappers (\textbf{Al} and \textbf{glossy}), replacing
        $T_x$ ($t_{\mathrm{first}}$) with $T_{\mathrm{CFD},x}$ ($\bar t_{\le 12.5\%}$)
        increases the linear timing slope by \SI{55}{\percent}--\SI{60}{\percent}---from
        \SI{1.71}{\ps\per\mm} to \SI{2.66}{\ps\per\mm} (and to
        \SI{3.23}{\ps\per\mm}--\SI{3.25}{\ps\per\mm} for the point quantile $t^{\mathrm{q}}_{12.5\%}$)---reducing
        the implied linear error from \SI{266}{\milli\meter} to
        \SI{184}{\milli\meter} in aluminium and from \SI{268}{\milli\meter} to
        \SI{181}{\milli\meter} in glossy paper (and from \SI{161}{\milli\meter}
        to \SI{158}{\milli\meter} in the bare plate).  This advantage is the
        value at the chosen constant fraction and is not a property of the
        wavefront time as such: with the scan of Fig.~\ref{fig:recon-alpha} the
        implied linear error of the glossy wrapper would be \SI{221}{\milli\meter}
        at $\alpha = \SI{5}{\percent}$ (a \SI{17}{\percent} gain over the
        first-hit time instead of \SI{32}{\percent}) and \SI{279}{\milli\meter}
        at $\alpha = \SI{50}{\percent}$, i.e.\ worse than the first-hit time
        itself.  Choosing $\alpha$ just below the ballistic fraction is what
        makes the wavefront time beat the first photon at all.
  \item For \textbf{TiO$_2$}, where $t_{\mathrm{first}}$ is artificially
        steepened by the $N_s^{-1/2}$ amplitude walk of the bright near-edge
        sensors, the optical CFD removes the amplitude-walk contribution,
        lowering the apparent slope magnitude from \SI{17.4}{\ps\per\mm} to the
        true propagation slope of \SI{15.2}{\ps\per\mm} while also reducing the
        rms timing noise from \SI{0.91}{\nano\second} to \SI{0.87}{\nano\second}.
\end{itemize}

Three improvements over an unweighted, non-symmetric $k$-NN baseline stand out:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The eight symmetries of the plate sharpen every timing channel.}
        Even before changing the timing observable, using the eight symmetries
        of the square plate (increasing the effective training density from
        \num{20000} to \num{160000} patterns) substantially reduces the
        first-arrival error, leaving \SI{23.8}{\milli\meter} in the bare

  \item \textbf{Optical CFD (\SI{12.5}{\percent}) outperforms first-arrival
        timing in all three reflective wrappers.}
        Adding the $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical CFD wavefront to capture both
        the arrival onset and the rising-edge slope $\Delta t_{\mathrm{rise},s}$
        lowers the timing-only error $\sigma_x$ further: from

        Appendix~\ref{app:recon} achieves the best resolution across the

        $\sigma_r = \SI{22.1}{\milli\meter}$, $r_{68} = \SI{20.7}{\milli\meter}$)

        \SI{15.7}{\milli\meter} ($\sigma_r = \SI{22.6}{\milli\meter}$,

\subsection{What this would require in practice}
\label{sec:recon-practice}

The quoted resolutions are those of an idealised optical read-out, and the
practical requirements are worth stating explicitly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Which electronics are needed depends on the wrapper.}  With a
        diffuse reflector the position comes from the light sharing, so the
        integrated charges of the eight channels suffice and no fast timing
        electronics are required (\SI{12.3}{\milli\meter} per axis from pulse
        integrals alone, \SI{11.9}{\milli\meter} combined).  With a specular
        wrapper the full-pulse integrals carry no position information and the
        localisation comes from the arrival-time wavefront and prompt-light
        share, requiring per-channel time digitisation (a TDC or fast waveform
        digitiser with $\lesssim\SI{100}{\ps}$ resolution) to reach
        \SI{15.7}{\milli\meter}--\SI{15.9}{\milli\meter} per axis.
  \item \textbf{The charge observable is the pulse integral, not the pulse
        amplitude.}  The numbers above use the integrated charge of the whole
        light pulse, which is what a charge-integrating ADC or slow shaper
        delivers.  A read-out recording only the peak of the shaped pulse would
        sample only the prompt subset of the light---\SI{53}{\percent} for the
        bare plate, \SI{33.7}{\percent} for TiO$_2$ and \SIrange{15}{16}{\percent}
        for the specular wrappers---and would incur higher Poisson noise.  On
        the other hand, a \emph{dual-gate} charge read-out (recording both a
        fast \SI{3}{\nano\second} prompt gate $N^{\mathrm{prompt}}_s$ and the
        full-event integral $N_s$) already achieves \SI{25}{\milli\meter}
        radial error in the specular wrappers even without sub-nanosecond
        timing.
  \item \textbf{Optical constant-fraction timing vs.\ first-photon timing.}
        While $t_{\mathrm{first}}$ is an extreme order statistic susceptible to
        single-photon shot noise and $N_s^{-1/2}$ amplitude walk, the
        $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical CFD wavefront $\bar t_{\le 12.5\%,s}$
        averages over the leading $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ of photoelectrons in the
        channel ($\sim 8$ photoelectrons in glossy paper and aluminium),
        making it directly analogous to a constant-fraction discriminator set
        near the leading edge of the prompt pulse and reducing the reconstruction
        error in all reflective wrappers.
  \item \textbf{The simulation contains no electronic imperfections.}  There is
        no SiPM saturation, electronic noise, dark-count background, optical
        crosstalk, gain spread or front-end time jitter, and the PDE is applied
        exactly as tabulated.  The reported resolutions therefore represent the
        fundamental optical limit of the config-1 geometry.
  \item \textbf{Symmetry-equivariant calibration in an experiment.}  Because the
        eight symmetries of the square plate are a geometric property of the
        detector, a real calibration scan with a reference muon hodoscope can
        apply the same eight-fold folding (Appendix~\ref{app:recon}), reducing
        the required calibration beam time by a factor of eight for a target
        statistical precision.
\end{itemize}

The practical conclusion is that a side-read-out muon trigger of this
geometry can be operated as a two-dimensional position detector at the
\SIrange{12}{18}{\milli\meter} level per axis (\SI{5}--\SI{7}{\percent} of the
plate width) without any modification to the optical hardware, provided the
read-out electronics and reconstruction metric are matched to the wrapper's
light-mixing regime.

\subsection{The bare configuration and the detection-efficiency threshold}

The statement about the \SI{80}{\percent} threshold is the one that can be
made quantitative without electronics, because the threshold enters the
detection efficiency only through the requirement that an event deposit more
than a certain number of photoelectrons. Writing $N_{\rm thr}$ for that
number, the simulated detection efficiency of Eq.~\ref{eq:de} is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DE}(N_{\rm thr}) = P\!\left(\sum_s N_s \;\ge\; N_{\rm thr}\right),
  \label{eq:de-sim}
\end{equation}
and the absolute value of $N_{\rm thr}$ corresponding to the experiment's
$400\,\mathrm{mV}$ is not known (it depends on the SiPM gain, the electronics
gain and the threshold setting). The statement itself, however, is
\emph{threshold-free}: it says that there \emph{exists} a working threshold at
which the three reflective treatments are above \SI{80}{\percent} and the bare
one is not.

Figure~\ref{fig:de} shows the simulated curves of Eq.~\ref{eq:de-sim} for the
four treatments of each layout, which have exactly the shape of the measured
detection-efficiency curves of Fig.~11 of Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}: the bare
plate's curve is shifted far to the left, and the three reflective curves are
nearly on top of one another. Table~\ref{tab:compareDE} lists, for each
layout, the window of thresholds in which the experiment's statement holds.
In the four directly-coupled layouts the window is non-empty and wide
($58$--$130$ photoelectrons for config~1, $26$--$56$ for Config 2 (mod)), and
it is empty only for config~4.
There the bare plate is now the weakest wrapper, as in the other layouts, but
its photoelectron distribution, set by the single SiPM behind the
wavelength-shifting fibres, overlaps TiO$_2$'s closely enough that no threshold
separates them at the \SI{80}{\percent} level; the experiment also reports no
bare config-4 measurement.

\subsection{The one disagreement: the position trend of the diffuse paint}

For three of the four treatments the simulation reproduces the measured sign of
the position trend in the four Table~IV layouts: the corner of the plate is
the dimmest place, as reported in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. The diffuse TiO$_2$
paint is the exception. In the four-sided layouts~1 and~2 the corner patch is
the \emph{brightest} of the three, a factor \num{1.75} above the centre, and
in the single-sided layouts~3 and~4 the centre is the brightest and the corner
the dimmest, which is the measured sign.

Config~2 (mod) is outside this comparison because its corner is instrumented.
There the corner patch is the brightest for all four treatments, including the
bare plate and the two specular wrappers (corner/centre ratios \num{2.65},
\num{3.58}, \num{1.21} and \num{1.17}). That is not a contradiction of the
measurement but a direct confirmation of the nearest-sensor rule: the paper's
corner is dim because it is far from every sensor, whereas here it lies about
\SI{27}{\milli\meter} from one.

The mechanism is the one of Sec.~\ref{sec:nearest}. A Lambertian paint is a
poor light mixer, so the response tracks the distance to the nearest sensor.
In a four-sided layout the corner patch is close to \emph{two} sensors and is
therefore the brightest, while the centre of the plate is the furthest point
from any of the eight sensors and is the dimmest---which is what the
three-patch scan of Table~\ref{tab:positions} shows
($6.398\times10^{-3}$ at the centre against $1.119\times10^{-2}$ at the
corner). Config~2 (mod) is the limiting case: its corner is close to
\emph{one} sensor, but the centre is \SI{168}{\milli\meter} from all four, so
the corner is still the brightest and the centre the darkest. In a
single-sided layout the same rule applies with a single collection site, and
there the centre of the plate is closer to the sensor than the far corners
are, so the sign of the trend is reversed.

The same rule explains why the paint is the \emph{least} uniform wrapper
($\kappa_{\rm 2D} = \num{1.91}$--\num{10.90}) while the specular wrappers are
nearly flat, and why the paint is the only wrapper whose light sharing
carries a first-order position signal. The disagreement with the measurement
is therefore not a random discrepancy: it is a single, identified difference in
how strongly the light is mixed, and it is confined to one treatment. The
experiment's own report does not state which of the two competing effects
dominates in the paint, so the discrepancy cannot be attributed from the
published data alone; in the model, the sign is set by the number of sensors
on the illuminated side of the plate.

\section{What the design knobs are worth: a controlled design study}
\label{sec:design}
% =====================================================================

Everything up to here varies the \emph{wrapper} of a fixed plate read out by
a fixed set of SiPMs. The builder of such a monitor faces a different
question: given a plate, a wrapper and a sensor budget, which change buys the
most? The wrapper comparison cannot answer it, because all of those runs share
one read-out layout and put the same treatment on all six faces. The four
knobs that the model actually exposes are the finish of the four \emph{narrow}
faces, the SiPM active area, the arrangement of the sensors, and a
wavelength-shifting fibre read-out on every face. This section isolates them.

Ten designs were run, each a single deliberate change from the v7 baseline,
\num{25000} whole-plate muons each, with identical beam, identical surface

The control reproduces the v7 glossy value of \num{520.7}~\pe/event to within
\SI{+9}{\percent}; the difference is the coupling grease, whose refractive
index is \num{1.55} here against the \num{1.46} default of the v7 runs
(\texttt{setCouplingIndex}). A higher grease index widens the escape cone at
the plate--pad interface and therefore raises the collected fraction, which is
what the \SI{9}{\percent} measures. The control is therefore the
denominator for every ``change'' statement below, not the v7 number. The
number of optical photons produced in the plate agrees across the ten designs
to within \SI{1.4}{\percent} (\num{19322}--\num{19598}), a spread that is
larger than the naive standard error of the mean but consistent with the
heavy tail of the muon energy-loss distribution rather than with any
difference in the optical model: the ten designs differ only in how the light
is collected after it is made.

\label{sec:design-yield}

The four knobs are not remotely equivalent
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designyield}). Blackening the four narrow faces costs
\SI{88}{\percent} of the light: \num{68.8}~\pe/event against
\num{568.2} for the control in layout~1, and \num{61.3} against \num{568.2}
in layout~8. This is not a small-area effect that one might expect from the
geometry---the narrow faces are only \SI{7.4}{\percent} of the
\SI{135000}{\milli\meter\squared} total surface---and the reason is the
optics rather than the area. A photon trapped by total internal reflection at
a large face necessarily travels at more than the critical angle
(\SI{39.3}{\degree}) from the plate normal, so its direction is dominated by
its in-plane component; and a photon whose in-plane direction is within
\SI{39.3}{\degree} of a narrow-face normal escapes at that face. For the
strongly trapped population the two conditions coincide over essentially every
azimuth, so the trapped light drains out of the narrow faces within a few
crossings, and a black narrow face converts the drain into an absorber. The measured
\SI{12}{\percent} that survives is the light that reaches a SiPM before it
makes that crossing.

Replacing the black faces with the diffuse paint recovers most of it but not
all: \num{339.6}~\pe/event, \SI{60}{\percent} of the control. The paint
returns \SI{96}{\percent} of each encounter, so a single bounce is nearly
free, but a Lambertian redirection sends the light back at a random angle and
each subsequent crossing carries a fresh chance of being absorbed in the paint
or lost elsewhere; the timing data of Sec.~\ref{sec:design-timing} confirm
that the light in this design makes many more crossings than in the
specular one. In light of this, the diffuse \emph{narrow} face is a
surprisingly expensive way to avoid a black one.

The SiPM area is the only knob with a near-unity return. Going from
\SI{4}{\milli\meter} to \SI{6}{\milli\meter} sensors multiplies the yield by
\num{2.11} in layout~1 and \num{2.09} in layout~8, i.e.\ it captures
\SI{94}{\percent} of the \num{2.25}-fold area increase. The missing
\SI{6}{\percent} is the lower photon flux at the outer edge of the larger
pad, and it is the same in both layouts, which is what one expects if the
mechanism is the local light field rather than the read-out.

The fibre read-out on every face is the design that changes the most at once.
With the glossy wrapper it delivers \num{220.3}~\pe/event with one SiPM per
side---\num{1.57} times the single-sided config~3 (\num{140.7}) and
\num{2.53} times the single-sided config~4 clamp (\num{87.0}) --- and
\num{273.0}~\pe/event when both ends of every fibre bundle are read out, a
further \num{1.24}. With the TiO$_2$ wrapper the same step is worth
\num{2.02} (\num{47.9} to \num{96.6}) and \num{1.19} (\num{96.6} to
\num{115.0}). The paint gains more from being read on every face because a
poorly mixing cavity loses most of its light to the faces that have no
collector; a fibre on every face removes that loss. None of the fibre designs,
however, comes close to the directly-coupled plate in absolute yield: the
best of them reaches \SI{48}{\percent} of the control, and even that is with
the mirror wrapper. The fibre path is expensive, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:design-timing} shows where the price is paid.

The spectra (Fig.~\ref{fig:designspectra}) show the second half of the story,
which the mean alone hides: filling the plate with collectors does not only
move the distribution to the right, it narrows it. The relative width of the
per-event yield is \num{0.25} for the control, \num{0.26} for the glossy
fibre designs, \num{0.27}--\num{0.28} for the diffuse-edge designs,
\num{0.38}--\num{0.40} for the TiO$_2$-wrapped fibre designs, and
\num{0.68}/\num{0.76} for the two black-edge designs. The absolute widths are
the opposite: \num{144} photoelectrons in the control against \num{47} in the
C1 black-edge design, because \SI{25}{\percent} of a large mean is larger
than \SI{68}{\percent} of a small one. A monitor that has to set a threshold
cares about the relative number, and there the black edge is the worst design
of the ten.

That is not counting statistics. With \num{68.8} photoelectrons the Poisson
term alone is \SI{12}{\percent}, so the observed \SI{68}{\percent} is
dominated by something else: the muon energy-loss tail, which contributes a
roughly common \SI{25}{\percent} to every design, plus a geometric term
specific to the ballistic read-out---where the track is, and how many
crossings it makes before a photon finds a sensor. The two terms add in
quadrature to the observed width, which puts the geometric term at
\SI{63}{\percent} for the C1 black-edge design. The same decomposition
explains why the TiO$_2$-wrapped fibre designs are wider (\num{0.38},
\num{0.40}) than the glossy ones (\num{0.26}): the paint is a poor mixer, so
the fraction of the light that reaches a fibre depends on where the track is,
whereas the mirror clamp averages it out.

The threshold-free form of the question asked by the paper tells the same
story (Fig.~\ref{fig:designde}). The threshold at which the detection
efficiency reaches \SI{90}{\percent} lies at \SI{78}{}--\SI{80}{\percent} of
the mean yield for six of the ten designs---both \SI{6}{\milli\meter} designs,
the control, the diffuse-edge design and the two glossy fibre designs---that
is, for every design whose relative width is \num{0.25}--\num{0.28}. The four
exceptions are the designs with the widest distributions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designspectra}): the black-edge designs reach
\SI{90}{\percent} at \SI{48}{\percent} and \SI{53}{\percent} of their mean,
the TiO$_2$-wrapped fibre designs at \SI{62}{\percent}. Setting a threshold is
therefore not a free way to reject noise in those four designs, because their
per-event light is spread too unevenly for a single cut to separate the bulk
of the distribution from its tail.

\subsection{Where the light goes}
\label{sec:design-maps}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig_opt_maps.pdf}
\caption{Collection efficiency over the plate for the campaign designs, in
\SI{5}{\milli\meter} cells and on one shared logarithmic colour scale (the
range is set by the 1st--99th percentile of the pooled cells so that a single
bright cell does not flatten the rest). Open squares mark the SiPM positions
of each design's layout. Cells with no statistics are drawn in the

normalised per panel.}
\label{fig:designmaps}
\end{figure}

The efficiency maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:designmaps}) put the two mechanisms of
Sec.~\ref{sec:nearest} side by side. The black-edge designs (top row) are the
purest form of the nearest-sensor rule found anywhere in this study: the map
is essentially the distance to the nearest SiPM, and the contrast is
\num{1.89} in layout~1 and \num{3.01} in layout~8, against \num{1.07} for the
same layout with a specular wrapper. Moving the eight sensors from
$u=\pm0.5$ to $u=\pm0.75$ (layout~8) makes the field \emph{less} uniform
(\num{1.89} to \num{3.01}), the opposite of what a naive ``spread the
sensors'' argument would predict, because the sensors move away from the
centre of the plate and the ballistic read-out has no way to help the middle.

The diffuse narrow faces (second group) leave the large faces glossy, and the
contrast stays at the specular floor: \num{1.14} in layout~1 with both
\SI{4}{\milli\meter} and \SI{6}{\milli\meter} sensors, \num{1.33} in
layout~8. A Lambertian \emph{side} face does not destroy the mixing that the
specular large faces provide, which is why this design is simultaneously the
brightest and one of the most uniform.

The fibre designs divide by wrapper in a way that the yields do not. With the
mirror clamp they have the flattest efficiency fields in the entire study,
\num{1.04} with four channels and \num{1.06} with eight---better than the
specular control (\num{1.07}) and better than any design of
Sec.~\ref{sec:uniformity}---because a fibre reads a \emph{line} rather than a
point: every point of the plate is at a similar distance from the collector
set, so the response no longer tracks a point-to-point distance. With the
TiO$_2$ wrapper the same read-out gives \num{2.15} and \num{2.26}: the paint
dominates, and no arrangement of collectors rescues a wrapper that does not
mix.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig_opt_maps_norm.pdf}
\caption{The maps of Fig.~\ref{fig:designmaps} normalised to each panel's own
plate-averaged efficiency, on a shared logarithmic scale. This view separates
\emph{how much} light is collected from \emph{where} it is collected: the

The normalised maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:designmapsnorm}) make the difference
explicit. In the ballistic designs the bright cells are the cells that contain
a sensor, as in the v6 maps of Sec.~\ref{sec:nearest}, and the cell between
two sensors on the same face is the darkest. In the four-sided fibre designs
the structure is a cross: four line collectors, two along each axis, and the
field is flat to \SI{6}{\percent} over most of the plate. This is the
geometric reason why a fibre read-out is the right choice when uniformity
matters more than yield.

\subsection{Timing: what the light costs on the way out}
\label{sec:design-timing}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/fig_opt_waveform.pdf}
\caption{Mean photoelectron arrival-time profile of the campaign designs, pooled
over all channels and divided by the number of events, so the ordinate is the
mean number of photoelectrons per \SI{0.1}{\nano\second} in an event. Left:
linear scale over the first \SI{25}{\nano\second}; right: logarithmic
ordinate over \SI{100}{\nano\second}, which is where the wavelength-shifting
tail lives.}
\label{fig:designwaveform}
\end{figure}

The narrow-edge finish is the timing knob
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designwaveform}, Table~\ref{tab:designres}). Blackening the
narrow faces makes the detector fast: \SI{65}{\percent} of the
photoelectrons arrive within \SI{3}{\nano\second} and \SI{90}{\percent}
within \SI{5.65}{\nano\second}, against \SI{15}{\percent} and
\SI{20.35}{\nano\second} for the control---a factor \num{3.6} in $t_{90}$. The
reason is the same drain that costs the light: the surviving photons are the
ones that reach a sensor directly, without the long random walk that a
reflective cavity imposes. The diffuse narrow faces sit in between
(\SI{25}{\percent}, \SI{11.85}{\nano\second}), consistent with a light path
that is lengthened but not eliminated.

The fibre designs are the slowest of the ten: \num{2.3}--\SI{5.4}{\percent}
within \SI{3}{\nano\second} and $t_{90}$ of
\SI{24}{\nano\second}--\SI{28}{\nano\second}, with
\num{91}--\SI{96}{\percent} of the photoelectrons created by
\gfour's \texttt{G4OpWLS} rather than by scintillation in the plate. That
fraction is the quantitative statement of the price noted in
Sec.~\ref{sec:design-yield}: the fibre designs are not scintillation counters
with a light guide, they are wavelength-shifting counters, and the
\SI{7}{\nano\second} exponential re-emission sets the resolution of their
timing. The glossy pair is systematically slower than the TiO$_2$ pair
(\SI{28.15}{\nano\second} against \SI{25.05}{\nano\second} at four
channels), because the specular clamp reflects the light back into the plate
at the mirror angle, so it has to randomise over several bounces before it can
enter a fibre, whereas the paint randomises the direction at the first
encounter.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Position resolution, and the trade-off the four knobs obey}
\label{sec:design-recon}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure~\ref{fig:designrecon}(a) applies the estimator of
Sec.~\ref{sec:recon} to each design's own channel pattern. The single-axis rms
errors fall into three groups that the yields alone do not predict. The two
\SI{6}{\milli\meter} designs are best, \SI{11.5}{\milli\meter} and
\SI{11.8}{\milli\meter}, with the control at \SI{15.9}{\milli\meter}; the
black-edge designs follow at \SI{18.8}{\milli\meter} and
\SI{17.4}{\milli\meter} and the diffuse-edge design at
\SI{19.9}{\milli\meter}. The four-sided fibre designs split by clamp: with the
white clamp they reach \SI{13.9}{\milli\meter} and
\SI{13.8}{\milli\meter} on a fifth of the control's light, while with the
mirror clamp they collapse to \SI{60.7}{\milli\meter} and
\SI{55.2}{\milli\meter}, barely better than the \SI{72}{\milli\meter} prior of
a plate that carries no position information at all. The statistical error of a
resolution measured on \num{5000} test events is
$\sigma_x/\sqrt{2N_{\rm test}}\approx\SI{0.2}{\milli\meter}$, so every
difference quoted here is significant.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/fig_opt_recon.pdf}
\caption{Left: single-axis position resolution obtained from each design's own
channel pattern with the combined estimator of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon}, on
\num{5000} held-out events. The dashed and dotted lines are the config-1
glossy (\SI{15.9}{\milli\meter}) and TiO$_2$ (\SI{11.9}{\milli\meter})

\label{fig:designrecon}
\end{figure}

The per-estimator decomposition separates three ways in which the light can
carry the position. Where the light is shared, the \emph{amplitude} does the
work: the white-clamp fibre designs reach \SI{13.9}{\milli\meter} and
\SI{12.4}{\milli\meter} with the pulse integral alone and are poor with timing
(\SI{30}{}--\SI{38}{\milli\meter} for the first arrival), because every
channel receives a share of every event and the shares themselves encode the
entry point. Where the light is ballistic the reverse holds: in the control and
the \SI{6}{\milli\meter} designs the pulse integral is the worst estimator
(\SI{46.2}{\milli\meter} and \SI{30.3}{\milli\meter}) and the arrival time the
best (\SI{17.4}{\milli\meter} and \SI{13.7}{\milli\meter}), as in the v6p5
plate of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon}. Blackening the narrow faces removes the late
light; with only prompt photons and between \num{8} and \num{9}
photoelectrons per channel the arrival-time spread is dominated by
photoelectron jitter rather than by the path length, so the timing estimators
degrade to \SI{26}{\milli\meter} and the design falls back on an amplitude that
sees one bright channel with large relative fluctuations. The mirror clamp is
the third case: it transports light from any entry point to the read-out
bundle, so each channel measures a line integral over the whole plate and
\emph{every} estimator fails (\SI{56}{}--\SI{69}{\milli\meter}). The linear
opposite-pair observable of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon} corroborates this from the
same data---its slope is \SI{8.7e-3}{} per millimetre with the white clamp and
\SI{4.3e-4}{} with the mirror, a factor \num{20}---so the collapse is a
property of the optics, not of the estimator.

Light therefore buys resolution only when it is partitioned. Within the
direct-coupled family the scaling is pure statistics: the \SI{6}{\milli\meter}
design improves the control's \SI{15.9}{\milli\meter} to
\SI{11.5}{\milli\meter}, against the \num{1.45} predicted by $\sqrt{N}$ for
\num{2.1} times the light (measured \num{1.39}), and the diffuse-edge design's
\num{340}~\pe\ predict \SI{20.6}{\milli\meter} against
\SI{19.9}{\milli\meter} measured. The white-clamp fibre design breaks that
scaling: \SI{13.8}{\milli\meter} with \num{115}~\pe\ is about \num{6.6} times
more position information per photoelectron than the control. Figure
\ref{fig:designrecon}(b) puts the two observables together; the Pareto front is
formed by the \SI{6}{\milli\meter} pair, the white-clamp fibre pair and the
black-edge pair, each at a different corner, and the control lies inside it.

\subsection{What to build}
\label{sec:design-what}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

Read together, the ten completed designs answer the builder's question in a way that
the wrapper study could not. One knob improves almost everything; the others
each buy a single property at a measurable price, and one buys nothing at all.

The SiPM area is the only free lunch: \num{2.1} times the light,
\num{1.4} times better position resolution---precisely the $\sqrt{N}$ gain
that the extra photons predict---and \num{1.8} times faster response, for
\num{2.25} times the active area, at a \SI{7}{\percent} higher exposure
contrast. Where the budget allows it, the larger sensor is strictly better on
everything except uniformity.

Blackening the narrow faces is the opposite: it is the fastest design in the
study, \num{3.6} times faster than the control, and it costs
\SI{88}{\percent} of the light, nearly triples the relative width of the
yield and gives the worst position resolution of the direct-coupled designs
(\SI{18.8}{\milli\meter} against \SI{15.9}{\milli\meter}). With
\num{69}~\pe/event it is still above the single-photoelectron threshold
everywhere on the plate, so the loss is affordable if the observable is time
rather than charge---it is the right choice for a detector that has to
timestamp a muon, not for one that has to count or image photons.

The diffuse narrow faces are dominated on every count: they cost
\SI{40}{\percent} of the light to the specular alternative, leave the relative
width slightly worse (\num{0.28} against \num{0.25}) and the resolution a
quarter worse (\SI{19.9}{\milli\meter}), and buy no uniformity. If the goal is
to avoid a black edge because the light is needed, the specular edge is better
in every observable measured here. The only argument for the paint is
mechanical---it is a diffuse reflector and does not need optical contact with a
mirror---and that argument is not tested by this campaign.

The fibre read-out on every face is the only design that improves yield and
uniformity together relative to its own single-sided counterpart
(\num{1.57} and \num{2.53} times the yield of configs~3 and~4, with the
flattest field of the study), and it holds the record for position information
per photoelectron (\num{6.6} times the control). It pays for that with the
slowest response in the study and with read-out hardware on all four faces.
The clamp finish is not a detail: the same geometry with a mirror clamp
collects more than twice as much light and loses the position information
entirely, so the diffuse clamp is what turns the fibre from a light guide into
a position sensor.

No design beats the control on all four observables. The \SI{6}{\milli\meter}
design dominates it on yield, timing and resolution and loses only on the
\SI{7}{\percent} contrast cost; the Pareto front of
Fig.~\ref{fig:designrecon} is the honest summary of the rest. The study
therefore does not discover a detector that is better than the one the
measurement describes in every respect---it quantifies what each deviation from
it would cost, which is the question a monitor designer actually asks.

\section{Caveats and open items}
\label{sec:caveats}

\paragraph{Config 4 collects less light than config 3, the opposite of the
measurement.}
With the nominal surfaces, config~4 reaches \num{87.0}~\pe/event against
\num{140.7}~\pe/event for config~3 in the glossy treatment, a factor \num{1.6}
too few, whereas Fig.~12 of Ref.~\cite{Rao2025} shows config~4 producing a
significantly \emph{higher} pulse height than config~3. The experiment reports
that the WLS-converted photons produce electrical signals with approximately
\SI{30}{\percent} efficiency, so a substantial loss in the wavelength-shifting
path is expected and is not reproduced here. The geometric cause is visible in
the maps: in config~4 the light has to reach a single SiPM through four
\SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} fibres, of which only the converged bundle is actually
read out, and the surviving light is then transported along the fibre to the
read-out at one end. The simulation does model the fibre, the multi-clad
index staircase, the far-end mirrors, the exponential re-emission and the
photocathode, but it does not model the finite acceptance of the fibre mouths
or the collection inefficiency of the re-emission at the fibre end, both of
which are candidate causes. A quantitative light budget---photons produced,
coupled into the fibres, surviving the transport, re-emitted within the
acquisition window, and detected---has not been made, and this is the largest
open item of the study. The comparison also inherits the caveat that the
experiment itself states, namely that the four configurations cannot be
compared directly because the number of parallel SiPMs changes the gain, the
input capacitance and the effective photon-coupling probability; the
experiment's config-4 value is a pulse height over a \SI{2500}{\milli\meter\squared}
fibre-augmented effective area, not a raw photoelectron count.

\paragraph{Two of the three reflector reflectances are fitted, not measured.}
Only the aluminium reflectance is taken from data (the evaporated-film
normal-incidence reflectance of McPeak \emph{et al.}~\cite{McPeak2015}). The
TiO$_2$ paint curve is a representative white-paint curve
($R \approx 0.955$--$0.983$) and the glossy paper curve
($R \approx 0.900$--$0.955$) is fitted to the statement that the two best
wrappers give nearly equal signals. The robustness of the reported
\emph{ordering} to a systematic shift of these curves has not been tested; the
ordering is expected to be robust, because the TiO$_2$ paint is separated from
the mirrors by the absence of TIR rather than by a difference in reflectance,
but this remains an untested assumption.

\paragraph{The WLS absorption length is a systematic band, not a scanned
parameter.}
The absolute strength of the Y-11 absorption is set by the absorption length
at \SI{430}{\nano\meter}, taken as \SI{1.0}{\milli\meter} with a plausible band
of \SIrange{0.3}{3}{\milli\meter}. The config-4 yield is directly sensitive to
it, and the band has not been scanned. The quoted config-4 numbers should
therefore be read with a systematic band of a few tens of per cent on the
yield.

\paragraph{Bare-holder contact is effective, not measured.}
Modelled with a real \SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} air gap between the polished plate
and the black holder ($f=0$), the bare configuration keeps the full TIR cone
but loses the entire sub-critical fraction at the black wall, and is the
\emph{third}-brightest wrapper in every layout---brighter than the paint,
dimmer than the two mirrors---not the weakest. (With the lower-bound
grease-backed foil it would sit second, since that foil falls below the bare
plate.) Reproducing
the experimental suppression requires the plate--holder
interface to be optically \emph{lossy}, i.e.\ partial real contact (frustrated
TIR). The extended contact scan shows that the crossing fraction is
$\approx\num{0.017}$ at the centre in every layout, \num{0.003}--\num{0.009} at
the edge/corner in configs~1--2, and \num{0.039}--\num{0.041} at the edge/corner
of config~3. No single $f$ matches every cell exactly; the production nominal
$f=\num{0.045}$ is the smallest sampled fraction that places all nine cells
below TiO$_2$, and the same nominal is applied to the bare faces of config~4,
which the scan does not cover but which at $f=\num{0.045}$ also falls below
TiO$_2$ (\num{19.5} versus \num{36.9}~\pe/event). The fraction is an effective
area fraction, not a measured holder gap, so $f=0$ is retained as the clean-air
reference and lower bound on the contact loss.

\paragraph{Position trend of the diffuse paint.}
As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}, the sign of the position trend of
the TiO$_2$ paint is layout-dependent in the simulation and disagrees with the
single trend reported in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}. The mechanism is identified
(the nearest-sensor rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:nearest}), but the simulation has not
been checked against an independent set of simulation settings, and the
experiment does not report the light-mixing behaviour that would decide the
issue.

\paragraph{Position reconstruction is limited by the optics, not by the estimator.}
Sec.~\ref{sec:recon} shows that the relative pattern of the eight SiPM
channels of config~1 localises the interaction point to
\SI{11.9}{\milli\meter} per axis (\SI{17.0}{\milli\meter} radially) with the
diffuse TiO$_2$ paint, \SI{15.7}{\milli\meter}--\SI{15.9}{\milli\meter} with
the specular wrappers (via $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical constant-fraction
timing and a symmetry-augmented regression), and \SI{19.4}{\milli\meter} with the
contact-limited bare plate.  However, the quoted numbers are those of an
\emph{optical} read-out: the simulation contains no SiPM
saturation, no electronic noise, no threshold, no gain spread, no dark counts
and no time jitter.  The timing channel additionally assumes a per-channel
time digitisation well below the \SI{0.5}{\nano\second} separations that carry
the signal, and the response function is learned from simulated events rather
than measured.  The reported resolution should therefore be read as an upper
bound for a real device.

\paragraph{Scale and normalisation.}
The absolute efficiencies are model-dependent: they are fixed by the assumed
PDE curve, the grease coupling, and the absorbed fraction of the tabulated
reflectivities. The SiPM breakdown voltage and gain (which drive the PDE) are
also temperature-dependent~\cite{Anfimov2021}, an effect not modelled here. The
robust results are the \emph{ordering} (glossy paper brightest and most uniform,
air-backed aluminium second, contact-limited bare plate weakest in every
layout), the
threshold statement of Table~\ref{tab:compareDE}, and the full-plate contrast
hierarchy, not the absolute efficiencies.

\paragraph{Optical-track stall (resolved).}
During earlier matrix production runs a rare stall was observed: a single worker
thread would enter a tight loop (100\% CPU) while the remaining threads waited at
the event-level barrier, freezing the run. It was traced to insufficiently
robust photon tracking in the \emph{tessellated} config-4 fibre solid, where
near-coincident facet boundaries could leave a track stuck. The production
config-4 geometry now uses the analytic, mitered \texttt{G4CutTubs} fibre legs
(Sec.~\ref{sec:wls}) instead, whose straight, planar miter interfaces eliminate
that class of degenerate boundary; the stall has not recurred since. As a safety
net the production workflow still wraps every task in a timeout with automatic
retry.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

A \gfour\ optical simulation of a
\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{250}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{10}{\milli\meter}
polished plastic scintillator read out by side-mounted SiPMs has been developed
and applied to a full matrix of five read-out layouts and four reflector
treatments at three beam positions (\num{150000} generated events), complemented
by a uniform whole-plate irradiation (\num{500000} generated events), a
\num{99000}-event bare-contact scan and an \num{18000}-event aluminium-backing
scan (\num{767000} generated events in total). The optical physics---unified surface model, finish selection,
photocathode PDE via the boundary process, and per-event photon
accounting---has been verified against the \gfour\ source.

The starting point of the study is an inversion: of the four surface treatments
compared in Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}, the bare polished plate is the only one that
returns no light to the plate---it is a total-internal-reflection guide whose
sub-critical cone is swallowed by the black wall---and yet it is the dimmest in
the measurement, below even the diffuse paint. In the model the bare plate is
the third-brightest wrapper even in the idealised case ($f=0$): the two
mirrors are brighter, because they keep the same TIR cone and additionally
hand the sub-critical
fraction back with a reflectivity of \num{0.90}--\num{0.96}, while the diffuse
paint is dimmer; the inversion is
therefore not a statement about the guide but about an extra loss channel. Two
unmeasured surface parameters had to be calibrated before the measured
ordering could be reproduced at all: the mean-field plate--holder contact
fraction, locked at $f=\num{0.045}$ as the smallest sampled value that places
all nine direct-coupled cells below TiO$_2$, and the optical backing of the
aluminium foil, taken as air because it reproduces the measured near-equality
of aluminium and glossy paper. With them, every layout reproduces the
experimentally observed ordering
\[
  \text{bare} < \text{TiO}_2 < \text{Al} < \text{glossy},
\]
glossy paper being the brightest and the most uniform wrapper, air-backed
aluminium within \SI{92}{\percent} of it, and the contact-limited bare plate
the weakest. A fifth layout, Config~2 (mod), truncates the four corners at
\SI{45}{\degree} and puts one sensor on each cut face; it keeps the same
ordering and within \SI{4}{\percent} the total light of config~2, but it does
not make the plate a better mixer. The measured statement that the bare
configuration is the only one to fail the \SI{80}{\percent}
detection-efficiency criterion is reproduced as well, and is shown to be a
statement about the \emph{threshold window} rather than about a single number.

Underneath the ordering there is a single mechanism, and it organises the rest
of the results. Two axes matter: the \emph{survival} of the trapped light,
which sets the yield, and the \emph{mixing} of that light, which sets the
uniformity of the response and the information content of the sensor pattern.
The specular wrappers win on both; the diffuse paint wins on neither, and
therefore is the dimmest wrapper but the one whose light sharing localises the
muon; the bare plate wins on mixing and loses on survival, and is the only
wrapper for which the two disagree. The unifying coordinate is the distance
from the interaction point to the nearest collection site: the response tracks
it whenever the cavity is not a perfect light mixer, which is why the full-plate
contrast runs from \num{1.07} (glossy) to \num{10.90} (diffuse paint in the
single-sided layout), and why the three-patch position trend of the paint
reverses its sign between the four-sided and the single-sided layouts. The
corner-cut layout is the direct confirmation: its instrumented corner is the
brightest patch for every wrapper and its centre is the darkest point of the
plate.

The same mechanism makes the eight channels of config~1 a position detector.
With a symmetry-augmented, relevance-weighted nearest-neighbour regression,
the diffuse paint fixes the interaction point to \SI{12.3}{\milli\meter} per
axis from light sharing alone (\SI{11.9}{\milli\meter} per axis and
\SI{17.0}{\milli\meter} radially when combined with prompt charge and timing,
i.e.\ \SI{5}{\percent} of the plate width), with no timing electronics needed;
the specular wrappers are useless in pulse integral (\num{44}--\SI{46}{\milli\meter})
but reach \SI{16.2}{\milli\meter} per axis from
$\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ optical constant-fraction timing alone and
\SI{15.7}{\milli\meter}--\SI{15.9}{\milli\meter} combined, given a fast enough
TDC. The contact-limited bare plate reaches \SI{19.4}{\milli\meter} per axis
when charge and timing are combined. These are the resolutions of the optical
read-out alone: the simulation contains no SiPM saturation, electronic noise,
threshold, gain spread or time jitter, and a real device would do worse. The WLS
read-out of config~4 is delayed by about \SI{4}{\nano\second} and green-shifted
to \SI{498.5}{\nano\meter} by the Y-11 fibres, confirming that the WLS path is
active; it is also the one configuration whose efficiency is not reproduced,
collecting a factor \num{1.6} less light than config~3 where the measurement
finds more, which remains the largest open item of the study.

Absolute efficiencies remain model-dependent (PDE, coupling, and tabulated
reflectivities); the robust results are the wrapper and layout orderings, the
threshold statement of Table~\ref{tab:compareDE}, the full-plate contrast
hierarchy, and the fact that the position information carried by a
side-read-out trigger depends on the wrapper rather than on the read-out
electronics.

result, and is kept out of the main text for that reason.

\subsection{The boundary process and the unified surface model}

Photon crossing of a dielectric interface is handled by
\texttt{G4OpBoundaryProcess}, which selects an \texttt{G4OpticalSurface} from the
unified surface model of Levin and Moisan~\cite{Levin1996} (whose key
parameters for Geant4/GATE photon transport were later
characterised~\cite{Nilsson2015}), using
(\emph{i}) a \texttt{G4LogicalBorderSurface} between two physical volumes or
(\emph{ii}) a \texttt{G4LogicalSkinSurface} on a logical volume. Reading the
source (lines 319--347), the lookup is: a border surface between the pre- and
post-step volumes is consulted first; if none exists, the skin surface of the
daughter (or, failing that, the mother) logical volume is used. This establishes
two facts on which the geometry relies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \texttt{G4LogicalBorderSurface} takes precedence over a
        \texttt{G4LogicalSkinSurface} at the same boundary---hence the grease
        and SiPM patches punch ``holes'' in the wrapping skin;
  \item if no surface object exists at a boundary, the process falls back to
        the default lossless TIR behaviour (via \texttt{DielectricDielectric()}).
\end{enumerate}

The boundary process requires a valid \texttt{RINDEX}: on medium~1 always, and
on medium~2 for a \texttt{polished}/\texttt{ground}
\texttt{dielectric\_dielectric} boundary (lines 289--306, 436--453);
\texttt{*backpainted} finishes instead read the backing index from the
\emph{surface} MPT (lines 356--374). If the property is absent the process sets
status \texttt{NoRINDEX} and kills the photon \emph{silently}---the message is
printed only above verbose level~1---which is why every material (and every
backpainted surface) in the model carries an explicit \texttt{RINDEX} table.
This silent kill is also why the far-end fibre mirror needed an explicit skin
(Sec.~\ref{sec:wls}): the metal cap has no \texttt{RINDEX}, so the un-covered
core-to-cap crossing was being lost.

For the \emph{unified} model the probability weights for the reflection
sub-models are read from the surface MPT---\texttt{SPECULARSPIKECONSTANT}
($f_{ss}$), \texttt{SPECULARLOBECONSTANT} ($f_{sl}$) and
\texttt{BACKSCATTERCONSTANT} ($f_{bs}$)---together with \texttt{REFLECTIVITY}
(line 381) and, where applicable, \texttt{TRANSMITTANCE} (line 394) and
\texttt{EFFICIENCY} (line 390). The surviving probability
$1 - f_{ss} - f_{sl} - f_{bs}$ is shared between diffuse (Lambertian) and bare
reflection.

\subsection{Reflector finishes}

The four treatments use three distinct code paths in
\texttt{G4OpBoundaryProcess}, which we verified:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{groundfrontpainted}} (TiO$_2$). The finish is handled by
        the ``painted-front'' branch (lines 462--488): a uniform random draw
        against \texttt{REFLECTIVITY} decides absorbed / transmitted / reflected,
        and reflection is set to a pure Lambertian distribution
        (\texttt{LambertianReflection}, line 481). There is \emph{no} TIR at this
        finish---photons are either diffusely reflected or absorbed. This is the
        physically correct description of sub-surface scattering in a thick
        TiO$_2$ paint.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{polishedbackpainted}} (Al, glossy paper). This finish is
        routed to \texttt{DielectricDielectric()} (line 456--459). The
        scintillator/backing interface still exhibits Fresnel reflection and TIR;
        sub-critical photons cross it and are returned by the backing ``paint''
        with reflectivity $R(\lambda)$ and a reflection distribution set by
        $f_{ss}, f_{sl}, f_{bs}$. We choose $f_{ss} = 1$ (pure specular spike),
        since both wrappers are smooth mirrors, and set the backing refractive
        index to distinguish the two physical situations. For the aluminium foil
        the backing index is the coupling grease ($n = 1.46$), following
        Ref.~\cite{Rao2025}, which applies grease so the foil optically contacts
        the plate; the critical angle is then only
        $\arcsin(1.46/1.58) = \SI{67.5}{\degree}$, so most light refracts out of
        the plate and is returned specularly by the foil, whose reflectivity is
        the tabulated normal-incidence reflectance of evaporated aluminium. For
        glossy paper the backing index is that of air ($n = 1.0003$), so the
        plate keeps its full TIR cone and only the sub-critical fraction reaches
        the paper, which acts as a slightly lossier mirror.
  \item \textbf{Bare plate}. This is deliberately \emph{not} given a wrapping
        skin. The plate keeps its plain polished boundary across a real
        \SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} air gap to the holder shell. The sub-critical
        fraction refracts across that gap and is then killed by the holder: the
        black shell carries an absorbing skin ($R = T = 0$), and its bulk
        (\SI{1}{\micro\meter} absorption length) would absorb the light anyway.
        Super-critical light is retained by TIR. There is \emph{no} absorbing
        skin on the plate itself; the same air-gap loss is applied at the
        read-out pads, which are separated from the plate by a thin air border
        in this configuration.
\end{itemize}

\section{The position-reconstruction machinery in detail}
\label{app:recon}
% =====================================================================

This appendix collects the formal definitions behind
Sec.~\ref{sec:recon}: the statistics of the first-arrival time, the two
constant-fraction estimators that are not used for the headline results, and
the full definition of the learned estimator.  It is reference material
rather than a result, and is kept out of the main text for that reason.

\subsection{The first-arrival time as an extreme order statistic}
\label{app:recon-timewalk}

The simplest timing observable is the first-photoelectron arrival time
$t^{\mathrm{first}}_s = t_{(1),s}$.  While convenient, $t_{(1),s}$ is an extreme
order statistic of a finite sample of size $N_s$ and suffers from two distinct
defects that act in opposite regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Order-statistic amplitude walk in light-sharing wrappers.}
        For the double-exponential scintillation pulse of
        Fig.~\ref{fig:opticaltiming} ($\tau_r = \SI{0.9}{\nano\second}$,
        $\tau_d = \SI{2.1}{\nano\second}$), the early photon arrival density
        above the ballistic wavefront $t_{0,s}$ grows linearly with time:
        \begin{equation}
          p_s(t) \approx \kappa\,(t - t_{0,s})\,\Theta(t - t_{0,s}),
          \qquad \kappa \approx \frac{1}{\tau_r\tau_d},
        \end{equation}
        so the cumulative distribution near the onset is quadratic,
        $F_s(t) \approx \frac{1}{2}\kappa(t - t_{0,s})^2$.  Drawing $N_s$
        independent photoelectrons yields a Weibull extreme-value law of index~2
        for the first arrival, $\mathbb{P}(t_{(1),s} - t_{0,s} > \delta)
        \approx \exp(-\frac{1}{2} N_s \kappa \delta^2)$, with mean and
        standard deviation
        \begin{equation}
          \mathbb{E}\bigl[t_{(1),s} - t_{0,s}\bigr]
          = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\kappa N_s}},
          \qquad
          \sigma\bigl(t_{(1),s}\bigr)
          = \sqrt{\frac{4-\pi}{2\kappa N_s}}.
          \label{eq:timewalk}
        \end{equation}
        Even at identical propagation distance, a channel that collects more
        photoelectrons triggers earlier by $\propto N_s^{-1/2}$.  In the
        diffuse TiO$_2$ wrapper, where a corner muon delivers $\sim 68$
        photoelectrons to the adjacent corner sensors and only $\sim 6$ to the
        opposite sensors, Eq.~\ref{eq:timewalk} shifts $t_{(1),s}$ earlier at
        the bright sensors by several hundred picoseconds purely from sample-size
        bias---the optical analogue of fixed-threshold amplitude walk.
  \item \emph{Extreme-tail shot noise in specular wrappers.}
        In the air-backed aluminium and glossy wrappers, total internal
        reflection mixes the integrated charge so thoroughly that $N_s \approx
        60\text{--}65$ is nearly identical across all eight channels, eliminating
        amplitude walk between channels.  There, however, $t_{(1),s}$ samples
        only the $1/N_s \approx \SI{1.5}{\percent}$ extreme tail of the arrival
        distribution and fluctuates from event to event depending on whether a
        single prompt photon enters the \SI{4}{\milli\meter}$\times$\SI{4}{\milli\meter}
        aperture on its first pass.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Variants of the constant-fraction estimator}
\label{app:recon-cfd}

Besides the leading-$\alpha$ wavefront mean of Eq.~\ref{eq:tcfd-mean}, two
further constant-fraction quantities are defined on the empirical cumulative
photoelectron distribution
$\hat F_s(t) = N_s^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_s}\Theta(t - t_{(j),s})$.
The first is the \emph{continuous $\alpha$-quantile arrival time}
$t^{\mathrm{q}}_{\alpha,s}$, obtained by piecewise-linear interpolation of the
order statistics at rank $u_s = \max(0,\, \alpha N_s - 1)$ with
$\gamma_s = u_s - \lfloor u_s\rfloor$:
\begin{equation}
  t^{\mathrm{q}}_{\alpha,s}
  = (1 - \gamma_s)\,t_{(\lfloor u_s\rfloor + 1),s}
    + \gamma_s\,t_{(\min(N_s,\,\lfloor u_s\rfloor + 2)),s}.
  \label{eq:tcfd-point}
\end{equation}
Its asymptotic variance
$\alpha(1-\alpha)/\bigl(N_s [p_s(t_\alpha)]^2\bigr)$ becomes large at distant
sensors, where the $\lceil\alpha N_s\rceil$-th photon falls in the low-density
trough between the ballistic peak and the reverberation tail; the wavefront
mean of Eq.~\ref{eq:tcfd-mean} averages over all photoelectrons in the
leading $\SI{12.5}{\percent}$ wavefront, where $p_s(t)$ is largest.  The
quantile time is quoted in Table~\ref{tab:reconobs} for the bare plate, where
it is marginally better than the wavefront mean; in the two specular wrappers
it is markedly worse, and it degrades steeply with $\alpha$ (from
\SI{193}{\milli\meter} at $\alpha = \SI{12.5}{\percent}$ to
\SI{660}{\milli\meter} at $\alpha = \SI{50}{\percent}$ in glossy paper,
against \SI{182}{\milli\meter} and \SI{279}{\milli\meter} for the wavefront
mean).

The second is the \emph{tapered $\mathrm{Beta}(1,3)$ wavefront L-estimator}
$t^{\mathrm{CFD}}_{\alpha,s}$, which weights the leading fraction
$\xi \in [0,1]$ (where $\xi = u / q_s$) with the smooth kernel
$\psi(\xi) = 3(1-\xi)^2$:
\begin{align}
  t^{\mathrm{CFD}}_{\alpha,s}
  &= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil q_s\rceil} w_j\,t_{(j),s}}
          {\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil q_s\rceil} w_j}, \nonumber\\[2pt]
  w_j &= \int_{(j-1)/q_s}^{\min(1,\,j/q_s)} 3(1-\xi)^2\,d\xi
       = \left(1 - \frac{j-1}{q_s}\right)^{\!3}
        - \left(1 - \min\!\left(1,\frac{j}{q_s}\right)\right)^{\!3}.
  \label{eq:tcfd-taper}
\end{align}
Because the weight $\psi(\xi)$ vanishes to second order at the boundary
$\xi = 1$, a photoelectron crossing the $\alpha N_s$ cutoff enters with
zero weight, making $t^{\mathrm{CFD}}_{\alpha,s}$ $C^2$-smooth with
respect to $N_s$ and eliminating integer-rank discretization jumps.  This
variant is the timing block of the \texttt{combined} estimator.

\subsection{The learned estimator in detail}
\label{app:recon-estimator}

The estimator of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon-learned-method} is a
\emph{symmetry-equivariant, relevance-weighted kernel $k$-nearest-neighbour
regression} in the standardised feature space, defined in four steps.

\paragraph{Event-centred feature blocks.}
For each event $e$, the eight-channel measurements are grouped into
translation- and yield-invariant blocks $\mathbf{f}^{(b)}_e \in \mathbb{R}^8$
($b = 1,\dots,B$), each centred on its per-event mean so that common-mode
fluctuations (total light yield $\ngam^{(e)}$ or global interaction time
$t_0$) cancel identically:
\begin{align}
  f^{\mathrm{amp}}_{e,s} &= \frac{N_{e,s}}{\sum_{q=0}^7 N_{e,q}} - \frac{1}{8}, &
  f^{\mathrm{prompt}}_{e,s} &= \frac{N^{\mathrm{prompt}}_{e,s}}{\sum_{q=0}^7 N^{\mathrm{prompt}}_{e,q}} - \frac{1}{8}, \nonumber\\[3pt]
  f^{\mathrm{time},(m)}_{e,s} &= t^{(m)}_{e,s} - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_e|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{A}_e} t^{(m)}_{e,q}, & &
  \label{eq:feat-blocks}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{A}_e = \{q : N_{e,q} > 0\}$ is the set of active channels
and $t^{(m)}_{e,s}$ denotes the timing observable ($t_{(1),s}$,
$t^{\mathrm{CFD}}_{12.5\%,s}$, or $\bar t_{\le 12.5\%,s}$).

\paragraph{Block-invariant standardisation.}
Each eight-sensor block $b$ is standardised using a single mean $\mu_b$
and standard deviation $\sigma_b$ pooled over all $8 N_{\mathrm{tr}}$
entries of that block in the training set:
\begin{equation}
  z^{(b)}_{i,s} = \frac{f^{(b)}_{i,s} - \mu_b}{\sigma_b},
  \qquad
  \mu_b = \frac{1}{8 N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\sum_{s=0}^{7} f^{(b)}_{i,s},
  \qquad
  \sigma_b = \left[\frac{1}{8 N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\sum_{s=0}^{7}\bigl(f^{(b)}_{i,s}-\mu_b\bigr)^2\right]^{1/2}.
  \label{eq:block-std}
\end{equation}
Pooling $\mu_b$ and $\sigma_b$ across the eight symmetric sensors of
block $b$ guarantees that spatial permutations of the eight sensors
commute with feature standardisation.

\paragraph{Dihedral group $D_4$ equivariance (eight-fold training augmentation).}
The square plate $[-L,L]^2$ and the config-1 sensor coordinates
$\{\mathbf{r}_s\}_{s=0}^{7}$ are invariant under the eight-element
dihedral group of the square,
\begin{equation}
  D_4 = \bigl\langle r, \sigma_x \;\big|\; r^4 = \sigma_x^2 = e,\;
        \sigma_x r \sigma_x = r^{-1} \bigr\rangle,
  \qquad |D_4| = 8,
\end{equation}
comprising the four rotations by $0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$
and the four reflections across $x=0$, $y=0$ and the diagonals $x=\pm y$.
Each orthogonal transformation $T_g \in D_4$ ($\det T_g = \pm 1$) maps
the sensor array onto itself via a bijection $\pi_g: \{0,\dots,7\}\to\{0,\dots,7\}$
defined by $\mathbf{r}_{\pi_g(s)} = T_g \mathbf{r}_s$, which induces an
$8\times 8$ orthogonal permutation matrix $\Pi_g$ ($\Pi_g^{\top}\Pi_g = I_8$)
acting identically on every feature block $\mathbf{z}^{(b)}$.
By physical symmetry, if $(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)$ is a valid
calibration event, then $(\Pi_g\mathbf{z}_i, T_g\mathbf{y}_i)$ is an
equally valid realization for all $g \in D_4$.  We therefore measure
the similarity between training event $i$ and test pattern $\mathbf{z}_t$
by the $D_4$-quotient distance
\begin{equation}
  d_w^2(i, t)
  = \min_{g \in D_4} \sum_{b=1}^{B} w_b\,\bigl\|\Pi_g \mathbf{z}^{(b)}_i - \mathbf{z}^{(b)}_t\bigr\|_2^2
  = \min_{g \in D_4} \sum_{b=1}^{B} w_b\,\bigl\|\mathbf{z}^{(b)}_i - \Pi_g^{\top}\mathbf{z}^{(b)}_t\bigr\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:d4-dist}
\end{equation}
recording the optimal symmetry element $g^*(i,t)$ that achieves the
minimum.  This expands the effective training sample eight-fold from
$N_{\mathrm{tr}} = \num{20000}$ to $8 N_{\mathrm{tr}} = \num{160000}$
patterns---reducing the characteristic nearest-neighbour distance in a
$d$-dimensional space by $8^{-1/d}$ and populating the corners and edges
uniformly---while the orthogonality identity on the right-hand side of
Eq.~\ref{eq:d4-dist} allows the eight permuted query vectors
$\Pi_g^{\top}\mathbf{z}_t$ to be precomputed in L1 cache so the
training table remains at its un-augmented size $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$.

\paragraph{Inverse-variance metric weighting and tricube kernel regression.}
When blocks of very different predictive power are concatenated with
equal weight ($w_b = 1$), uninformative blocks (such as the pulse
integrals in a specular wrapper, where $\sigma_{r,\mathrm{amp}} \approx
\SI{63}{\milli\meter}$) contribute eight dimensions of pure Poisson
variance to Eq.~\ref{eq:d4-dist} and corrupt the neighbour ranking.
Denoting by $\sigma_{r,b}$ the single-block radial rms error and
$\sigma_{r,\min} = \min_{c}\sigma_{r,c}$, each block in the combined
estimator is weighted by its inverse fourth-power error (squared
inverse MSE):
\begin{equation}
  w_b = \left(\frac{\sigma_{r,\min}}{\sigma_{r,b}}\right)^{\!4}.
  \label{eq:block-weights}
\end{equation}
This automatically suppresses noisy blocks ($w_{\mathrm{amp}} = \num{0.017}$
in glossy paper and $w_{\mathrm{time}} = \num{0.088}$ in TiO$_2$) while
retaining all blocks at comparable weight ($w_b \in [\num{0.76}, 1]$) in
the bare plate where every channel contributes.  From the $k=40$ nearest
symmetry-matched training events $\{(i_m, g^*_m)\}_{m=1}^{k}$ with
sorted distances $d_1 \le d_2 \le \dots \le d_k$, the interaction
position is reconstructed via a Nadaraya--Watson estimator with a
compact tricube kernel:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t
  = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{k} K\!\left(\dfrac{d_m}{h_t}\right) T_{g^*_m}\mathbf{y}_{i_m}}
         {\sum_{m=1}^{k} K\!\left(\dfrac{d_m}{h_t}\right)},
  \qquad
  K(u) = \bigl(1 - u^3\bigr)^3\,\Theta(1 - u),
  \qquad
  h_t = 1.05\,d_k.
  \label{eq:knn-kernel}
\end{equation}
